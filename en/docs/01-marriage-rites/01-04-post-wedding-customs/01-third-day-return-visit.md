# Third-Day Return Visit

![婚后礼俗时序图 / Timeline of post-wedding customs](../../../../assets/diagrams/婚后礼俗时序图.svg)

*Figure 3. The sequence of post-wedding custom: return visit — thanking the matchmaker — sending lamps — full month and hundredth day — acknowledging and visiting kin — division of the household.*

## 1. Definition

“Returning to the door”, also called “gui ning” (homecoming), the “joint return” or “returning to the mother’s house”, is the rite in which **after the wedding the bride (or the couple together) returns to her parents’ home**. The customary time is the third day after the wedding, whence “third-day return” (with variants of the second, fourth or seventh day).

**Essentials**:
- The return is the **closing rite** of the wedding — after being “taken in at the door”, the bride returns to her parents’ house as a “guest”, completing the ceremonial confirmation of her change of status.
- The “**joint return**” means the couple go together; the “**single return**” means the bride goes alone (in some regions).
- The return has its **rules**: it must be made on the same day or the next, staying overnight at the parents’ house being avoided (in some regions, to preserve the sense of “no empty room”).

## 2. Historical Origins

- **Pre-Qin**: the *Shihunli* has the sections “the wife is presented to the parents-in-law” and “the parents-in-law feast the wife”, the beginning of post-wedding custom; the rite of “gui ning” appears in the *Shijing* (“to go home and greet my parents”).
- **From the Tang and Song**: the custom became general, and the gazetteers commonly record the “third-day return” and the “seventh-day return”.
- **Ming and Qing**: the ceremony settled into fixed form, with fixed rules for gifts and propriety (much recorded in the “Customs” sections of Ming and Qing gazetteers).
- **Today**: the return is generally kept in town and country (called “huimen”, “returning to the mother’s house”, “staying at the mother’s house”), the time being chiefly the third day or the next day.

## 3. Ceremonial Procedure

1. **Preparing gifts**: the husband’s family prepares the return gifts, commonly cakes, wine, meat, sweets and fruit (pairs being auspicious).
2. **The homecoming**: the couple (or the bride) return to the mother’s house on the third day and bow to her parents.
3. **The banquet**: the mother’s house holds a banquet, with close kin invited as company (the “return banquet”, the “gathering of kin”).
4. **Gifts in return**: the mother’s house gives clothing, shoes and socks, food and other “return gifts”.
5. **Returning**: they return to the husband’s house on the same day or the next, overnight stays at the mother’s house being avoided (in some regions).

## 4. Regional and Ethnic Variants

- **North China**: mostly the “third-day return”, the bride going back to the husband’s house the same day, otherwise there being the taboo of an “empty room”.
- **Jiangnan**: called “huimen”, ceremonious, the return gifts mostly food and cloth.
- **Fujian and Taiwan**: called “returning to the outer house” or “being a guest”, ceremonious, in some places on the second day after the wedding.
- **Guangdong**: called “huimen” or the “third-morning return”, famous for the custom of returning with a roast pig (see also the entry “The Roast Pig Ritual”).
- **Ethnic minorities**: some peoples have a different timing and standard for the post-wedding homecoming (as among peoples practising “not taking up residence in the husband’s house”, where the homecoming period is long); these are not the Han return visit and must be recorded separately.

## 5. Objects and Documents

- **Return gifts**: cakes, wine, meat, sweets and fruit (in pairs).
- **Return gifts given back**: cloth, shoes and socks, food, red packets.
- **Related documents**: the gift list (the list of return gifts) and invitations (for the return banquet).
