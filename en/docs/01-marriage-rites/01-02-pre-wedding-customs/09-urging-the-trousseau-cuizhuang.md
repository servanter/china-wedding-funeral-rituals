# Urging the Trousseau (Cuizhuang)

## 1. Definition

Urging the trousseau, also called the “urging gift” or “urging verses”, is the custom by which, as the wedding date approaches, the groom’s family sends gifts to the bride’s family to hasten her preparations.

Its name is “urging” but its substance is “courtesy”: ostensibly a hurry, in fact **a last-stage renewal of good will, a sending of things and a keeping in touch** before the wedding. Only a few days normally separate the urging from the reception, so it is a transition between negotiation, engagement, preparation and the wedding itself.

## 2. Historical Origins

- The custom is already found in the Tang. Before a Tang wedding the man’s family sent “urging” things, and there were “urging verses” — poems by relatives and friends urging the bride to dress, an elegant practice of literary weddings.
- The Song *Dongjing menghua lu*, “Taking a Wife”, and *Mengliang lu*, “Marriages”, record that before the reception the man’s family sent an “urging” gift, with “urging verses” and “urging music”.
- In the Ming and Qing the form varied from place to place: in some places the urging gift was the “urging box” (holding powder, rouge, flowers and a mirror), in others “urging wine”.
- In some regions the urging was combined with the “sending of the dowry”: the man’s family sent the urging gift, and the woman’s family at once sent out the trousseau.
- In contemporary weddings this stage has largely disappeared, or been absorbed into the “sending of the dowry” and the “gift to the bride’s family”.

## 3. Ceremonial Procedure

1. **Preparing the gift**: the groom’s family prepares the urging gift, commonly powder and rouge, scented soap, a mirror, a comb, flowers, supplementary jewellery, wine and cakes.
2. **Sending the urging**: it is taken to the bride’s house by the matchmaker or an elder woman of the groom’s family, usually three days or one day before the reception.
3. **Urging verses (or writing)**: in some places urging verses or an urging note are enclosed, the wording hastening the bride to dress — the “urging verse” often with a jocular tone of “make haste to dress”.
4. **Receiving the gift**: the bride’s family receives it and returns kerchiefs, stationery and the like.
5. **Sending out the trousseau**: in some places the bride’s family, on receiving the urging gift, at once sends the dowry to the groom’s house.
6. **Preparing for the reception**: both sides make their last preparations and await the day.

## 4. Regional and Ethnic Variants

- **North China**: called “cuizhuang” or “sending the urging box”, chiefly combs, mirrors, powder and rouge.
- **Jiangsu and Zhejiang**: called “cuizhuang”, usually one or two days before the reception, with “urging verses”.
- **Fujian and Guangdong**: called the “urging gift”, generally combined with the “sending of the dowry” — the gift panniers go to the bride’s house and the dowry is carried back at once.
- **Hakka areas**: called “cuizhuang”, with insistence on “sending powder and a mirror”.
- **Ethnic minorities**: some peoples have a stage of “sending gifts” or “urging the marriage” before the reception, such as the Miao “urging the marriage after the betrothal gift” and the Yi “urging wine”. Their names and contents, however, mostly follow the self-designation of the group, and the Han name “urging the trousseau” is not generally used.

## 5. Objects and Documents

- **The urging box**: a box holding powder and rouge, a comb, a mirror and flowers.
- **Urging verses or the urging note**: documents.
- **Wine and cakes**: accompanying the gift.
- **Kerchiefs and stationery**: the bride’s family’s return.
