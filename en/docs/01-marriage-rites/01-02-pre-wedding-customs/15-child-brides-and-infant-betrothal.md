# Child Brides, Prenatal Betrothals and Infant Betrothals (A Critical Survey)

## 1. Definition

This entry examines three kinds of “malformed marriage” in the old order:

- **The child bride**: a girl sent in early childhood to be raised in the boy’s household, and married to him when grown. Also called the “fostered daughter-in-law”.
- **The prenatal betrothal**: the parents of two families agreeing, before the child is born, that if the child should be a boy or a girl they shall be married.
- **The infant betrothal**: an engagement arranged by the parents while the children are still small (or unborn).

What the three have in common is that **the parties had no opportunity whatever to express their will**, and that the motive for the engagement was usually not the marriage itself but the economic, labour or social needs of the families. **This entry is a critical survey and does not constitute an advocacy of these customs.**

## 2. Historical Origins

### The child bride

- Of very early origin, with clearer records from the Song, and extremely widespread in the Ming and Qing, especially in the poor mountain regions of the south.
- Causes:
  1. **From the man’s side**: the bride-price is saved, and a free labourer is gained (a child bride had to do housework and field work).
  2. **From the woman’s side**: poverty made it impossible to raise a daughter; sending her out lightened the burden, and no dowry had to be prepared.
- The child bride had extremely low status: no dowry, no wedding ceremony, often ill-treated, and her marriage lacked ritual legitimacy (there being no Six Rites).
- Investigations of the late Qing and early Republic, and the rural surveys of Republican-era sociologists (including the materials gathered in *A Gazetteer of Chinese Customs*), record the plight of child brides in abundance.
- Although the *Civil Code*, “Part on Kinship”, of 1930 established freedom of marriage, the practice persisted in the countryside into the middle of the twentieth century.

### Prenatal and infant betrothals

- The prenatal betrothal is found chiefly where gentry or lineages needed an alliance; motives of “repaying a kindness” or “an old family friendship” also occur.
- The infant betrothal was the commonest in popular practice, its motives being to seize a good match of equal standing, to save on the future bride-price, or to serve relations between the two families (such as mutual help between in-laws).
- The risk was very great: if either party grew up not matching expectations in appearance, character or health, or with a disability, the engagement was still hard to dissolve, causing lifelong suffering.

### Institutional reflection

- The essence of these customs is **the treatment of marriage as an allocation of resources between families**, with the parties as objects of exchange.
- Modern law expressly prohibits them:
  - Article 1042 of the Civil Code: “Arranged marriages, mercenary marriages and other acts interfering with freedom of marriage are prohibited.”
  - Article 1047: the statutory age of marriage (not earlier than twenty-two for a man, twenty for a woman).
  - Article 1046: marriage must be entirely voluntary on both sides.
- In a few places today there has been a revival of “infant betrothals” and “early marriage”, which must be resisted through the rule of law and through education.

## 3. Ceremonial Procedure

### The child bride

1. **Sending the child**: the girl, in early childhood (often a few years old), is sent to the boy’s house, usually without any formal ceremony.
2. **Raising**: she lives and works in the boy’s household, the relation with her “parents-in-law” being unequal from the outset.
3. **Consummation**: when both have grown (usually when the girl is about fifteen), a simple rite of “rounding the chamber” is held — no Six Rites, no bridal palanquin, no great feast.
4. **Supplementary rites**: some families perform a simplified wedding ceremony at the consummation.

### Prenatal and infant betrothals

1. **The agreement**: the parents agree, usually by written or oral oath, or with an object as token (“cutting the lapel”, “exchanging cards”).
2. **Relations**: the two families address one another as in-laws and exchange visits at festivals.
3. **Marriage on maturity**: when the children are grown they marry as agreed, still performing the Six Rites (usually simplified).
4. **Disputes over repudiation**: when one side is unwilling, serious disputes commonly follow, requiring the mediation of lineage elders or the magistrate.

## 4. Regional and Ethnic Variants

- **The southern mountain regions (Hunan, Jiangxi, Fujian, Guangdong, Guangxi)**: child brides were the commonest, directly connected with poverty and the mountain economy.
- **North China**: infant betrothals were more common, with “engagement at three” and “exchange of brides” (the daughters of two families exchanged as wives).
- **Some regions**: there were still more deformed forms, such as the “waiting-for-a-brother girl” (a girl sent to a family that had no son yet, to wait for a boy to be born, and who might wait all her life), and the “exchange” and “rotation” of brides.
- **Ethnic minorities**: some peoples have customs of “early marriage” or “betrothal of infant sons”, their social basis lying in the traditional institutions of the group (inter-lineage marriage, tribal alliance), which differ from the causes of the Han child bride, born of poverty; they cannot be treated as one. Whatever the cause, practices involving the marriage of minors conflict with the modern rule of law.

## 5. Objects and Documents

- **Oaths of betrothal, the cut lapel, exchanged cards**: the tokens of prenatal and infant betrothals.
- **The simple rite of “rounding the chamber”**: the meagre ceremony at the marriage of a child bride, without fixed form.
- **Social survey reports**: the rural surveys of the Republican period record the plight of child brides and are important primary materials.
