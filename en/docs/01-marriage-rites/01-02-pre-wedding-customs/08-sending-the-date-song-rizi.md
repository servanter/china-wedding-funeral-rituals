# Sending the Date (Song Rizi)

## 1. Definition

Sending the date, also called “putting down the date”, “notifying”, “sending the marriage card” or “sending the day”, is the custom by which the groom’s family, having chosen the wedding date, notifies the bride’s family in writing on a “date card”.

**The boundary with the entry “The Six Rites / Qingqi”**: qingqi is the rite prescribed by the ritual classic, in which an envoy with a goose “requests” the bride’s family’s approval, the tone giving the initiative to the bride’s side; sending the date is the later popular form, in which the groom’s family, having fixed the date, “sends” it to the bride’s family, the tone giving the initiative to the groom’s. This shift from “requesting” to “sending” reflects precisely the reality of the husband’s family’s initiative after the Song.

## 2. Historical Origins

- The words of qingqi in the *Shihunli* are “he has sent me to request an auspicious day”, and the host answers “I will hear and obey nothing else”.
- Zhu Xi’s *Family Rituals* simply changes “requesting the date” to “announcing the date”, on the ground that the date having been divined there is no need to request it.
- In the Ming and Qing “sending the date” became a fixed stage, as in *Qing bai lei chao* and the provincial gazetteers. The “date slip” was written on red paper folded double, setting out the auspicious day and hour and the zodiac animals and taboos to be avoided.
- The bases of date selection were chiefly the yellow-path auspicious days of the *Xieji bianfang shu* tradition, the “zhoutang” diagram (a diagram of good and ill fortune for weddings) and the birth characters of both parties.
- Popular date selection has many taboos: a “year without spring” (a widow’s year) is avoided, as is a date clashing with the parents’ birth data, the “Sanniang sha”, and the third and seventh lunar months (Qingming and the Ghost Festival).
- Contemporary date selection is mostly a matter of “choosing a holiday”, and the influence of the traditional taboos is much weakened.

## 3. Ceremonial Procedure

1. **Choosing the day**: a master of yin and yang is engaged, or the almanac consulted, to fix the wedding date, with an eye to the farming calendar, the solar terms and the circumstances of both families.
2. **Writing the date card**: the “date card” (marriage card) is written on red paper, setting out:
   - the auspicious day and hour of the reception;
   - the hour for mounting the palanquin (or car);
   - the zodiac animals to be avoided (“those born in the year of X are not to attend”);
   - other taboos (such as pregnant women not accompanying the bride).
3. **Sending the date**: the matchmaker or a messenger takes the date card to the bride’s house, with ceremonial cakes and sweets.
4. **Receiving the date**: the bride’s family receives the card; if it objects to the date, the matchmaker may mediate another.
5. **Preparing the trousseau**: on the strength of the card the bride’s family prepares the trousseau and hurries the making of the wedding clothes, entering the stage of “preparing the marriage”.
6. **Informing relatives and friends**: both families inform their kin and send out invitations.
7. **Engaging helpers**: the hands needed for the wedding are engaged (kitchen, masters of ceremony, drums and music).

## 4. Regional and Ethnic Variants

- **North China**: called “song rizi”, “xia rizi” or “tongxin”; the date card insists on a “double month and double day”.
- **Jiangsu and Zhejiang**: called “tonghua”, “song quitie” or “dingri”; in some places it must be sent three times (“first sending, second sending, third sending”).
- **Southern Fujian**: called “song ritou”; the “day slip” is sent to the bride’s house with the ceremonial cakes.
- **Guangdong**: called “fixing the day after the major gift”, generally done together with the major gift.
- **Ethnic minorities**: the mechanism by which the date is fixed differs. Among the Yi the date must be decided by “notching wood” and “chicken divination”; among the Dai it is settled by the village elders and the temple; among the Miao weddings are often held collectively during festivals (such as the Miao New Year and the Sisters’ Festival). These mechanisms rest on village and religious authority, and differ from the Han family’s choice of a day.

## 5. Objects and Documents

- **The date card (marriage card)**: the central document, written on red paper.
- **Ceremonial cakes and sweets**: sent with the date card to the bride’s house.
- **Almanacs and books of date selection**: the *Xieji bianfang shu*, *Yuxia ji* and the like.
- **Invitations**: for informing relatives and friends.
- **The zhoutang diagram**: a diagram used specifically for choosing wedding days.
