# Preparing and Delivering the Dowry

## 1. Definition

The dowry (trousseau, portion) is the property prepared by the bride’s family for their marrying daughter: furniture, bedding, chests, clothing, jewellery and household vessels, in some cases including land, houses and shops (“portion land”, “trousseau land”).

Delivering the dowry is the rite of carrying the trousseau from the bride’s house to the husband’s, usually on the day before or the day of the reception. It is one of the most spectacular sights of a Chinese wedding (as with the “ten *li* of red dowry” of Ninghai in Zhejiang).

## 2. Historical Origins

- The *Shihunli* has no word for “dowry”, but records the accompanying concubines (nieces and younger sisters accompanying the bride) and the rite of “feasting the escort”, an early ritual form of the dowry system.
- After the Han the dowry grew steadily larger. The Tang *Code with Subcommentary* provides for the ownership of the trousseau: “property obtained from the wife’s family is not part of the division” — the dowry being the wife’s private property, not subject to partition in the husband’s household. This is an important legal feature of the dowry system.
- The Song *Dongjing menghua lu*, “Taking a Wife”, records the woman’s family “furnishing the chamber” before the wedding — that is, sending the dowry to the husband’s house to fit out the bridal room.
- In the Ming and Qing the dowry was at its height, “heavy dowry” being the fashion and even a burden on families; the gazetteers lament that “marrying off a daughter ruins the household”.
- The “ten *li* of red dowry” of Ninghai in Zhejiang is the extreme form of the system: from furniture and vessels to bedding and dressing implements, the whole carried in a procession stretching ten *li*.
- Contemporary dowries are mostly electrical appliances, furniture, a car or cash: the form has changed, the function (to give the daughter material security in her new household) has not.

## 3. Ceremonial Procedure

1. **Preparing the trousseau**: the bride’s family begins preparing from the engagement, including:
   - furniture (bed, cupboards, tables, chests);
   - bedding (wedding quilt and pillow);
   - chests (camphor chests, the “bucket of descendants”);
   - clothing, jewellery, shoes and hats;
   - daily necessities (mirror, comb, basin, pail, lamp).
2. **Packing the panniers**: the trousseau is packed into chests and carrying boxes, plastered with red paper and bound with red cord, and a “trousseau inventory” compiled.
3. **Sending out the trousseau**: it sets out from the bride’s house at an auspicious hour (usually the day before the reception), carried by bearers and accompanied by drums and music.
4. **Order of the procession**: the rule is “large pieces before small”, “heavy before light”; in some places the “bucket of descendants” goes first (in the sense of “an early noble son”).
5. **Customs along the way**: firecrackers are set off and lucky coins scattered; at bridges and temples firecrackers are set off “to press down evil”; in some places the procession must “go round the streets” in display.
6. **Furnishing the chamber**: when the trousseau reaches the husband’s house, the “lady who brings the trousseau” from the bride’s family, or a woman of full fortune, spreads it out in the bridal room — “furnishing the chamber”.
7. **Checking the inventory**: the husband’s family checks the items against the inventory, and the two sides sign off on the handover (in some regions).
8. **Tips**: the husband’s family distributes tips to the bearers and to those who furnished the chamber.

## 4. Regional and Ethnic Variants

- **North China**: called “song jiazhuang” or “guo jiazhuang”; the “carrying boxes” and “carrying chests” are insisted upon, and the longer the procession the greater the credit.
- **Jiangsu and Zhejiang**: the “ten *li* of red dowry” of Ninghai is a national-level item of intangible heritage; elsewhere “displaying the trousseau” is the rule.
- **Fujian and Guangdong**: the trousseau includes the “bucket of descendants” (chamber pot, foot basin, water pail), with dates, peanuts and red eggs inside.
- **Hakka areas**: “portion land” is insisted upon — land as the marriage portion, the daughter’s private property being the produce of that land.
- **Ethnic minorities**: the content of the dowry differs widely. The Dai dowry includes silver belts and silver ornaments; the Miao, silver ornaments and brocade; the Mongolian, livestock; the Tibetan, jewellery, *pulu* cloth and livestock. Some peoples practise uxorilocal marriage, in which the man sends labour or property to the woman’s family — the reverse of the Han dowry.

## 5. Objects and Documents

- **Chests**: the camphor chest is typical, moth-proof and durable.
- **The bucket of descendants**: chamber pot, foot basin and water pail, with dates and chestnuts inside (in the sense of “an early standing son”).
- **Wedding quilt and pillow**: embroidered with dragons and phoenixes, mandarin ducks and pomegranates.
- **The trousseau inventory**: listing the items, checked off and handed over by both sides.
- **Carrying boxes, poles and ropes**: the implements of carriage.
- **The lamp (the ever-burning lamp)**: an indispensable piece of the trousseau in some regions, lit after the bride enters the husband’s house.
