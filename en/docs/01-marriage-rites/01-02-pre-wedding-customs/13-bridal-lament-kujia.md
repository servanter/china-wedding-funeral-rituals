# Bridal Lament (Kujia)

![哭嫁 / The bridal lament](../../../../assets/images/17-bridal-lament.png)


## 1. Definition

Bridal lament is the custom in which a woman, before her wedding (and when mounting the palanquin), expresses her grief at parting through weeping and singing. The weeping is **weeping prescribed by custom**, not a loss of composure: not to weep was reckoned inauspicious (“without weeping there is no liveliness”; “without weeping the house will not prosper”).

The words of the bridal lament are called “bridal lament songs” or “lament tunes”, with fixed patterns of line, rhyme and content structure, and constitute an important genre of oral literature.

## 2. Historical Origins

- The early form of bridal lament is linked to the idea of “the sorrow of marriage”: the ancient ritual expressed the pain of parting by “the house that marries off a daughter keeps the lamps burning for three nights” (*Liji*, “Zengzi’s Questions”).
- The *Liji*, “Zengzi’s Questions”, records Confucius: “The house that marries off a daughter keeps the lamps burning for three nights, thinking of the parting.” This is the earliest textual basis of the emotional foundation of bridal lament.
- From the Tang and Song bridal lament flourished especially in the south. Ming and Qing gazetteers record it abundantly.
- Its distribution is centred on the **Tujia, Miao, Dong, Zhuang, Yao, Yi, Hani and (in part) Tibetan** peoples, and on the southern Han provinces (Hunan, Hubei, Sichuan, Guizhou, Guangxi, Guangdong); it exists in the north too, but in simpler form.
- Today it survives in some rural areas and has become an object of intangible cultural heritage protection.

## 3. Ceremonial Procedure

Bridal lament runs through several stages before the wedding, and may be divided into several “points of weeping”:

1. **Lament of preparation**: beginning when the engagement or the date is settled, sung daily, largely on attachment to life in her parents’ house.
2. **Lament at the coiffure**: sung on the night of the coming-of-age coiffure, turning to gratitude for her parents’ care.
3. **Lament of leave-taking from the ancestors**: sung when bowing farewell to the ancestors.
4. **Lament of leave-taking from kin**: sung in turn to parents, brothers, sisters and sisters-in-law (“weeping for father and mother”, “weeping for brother and sister-in-law”, “weeping for the sisters”).
5. **Lament on mounting the palanquin**: sung as she mounts the palanquin (or car), mother and daughter weeping together — the most intense passage of the whole.
6. **Lament of farewell to the neighbours**: taking leave of the neighbours along the way, in some places the neighbours weeping in reply.

### Structure of the verses

- The opening line is usually a cry (as “O my father, O my mother”).
- The middle section is narrative and lyrical: recalling life in her parents’ house, thanking them for raising her, charging her brothers and sisters-in-law to look after them.
- The close is usually blessing and farewell.
- Rhyme, comparison and allusion are attended to; a good weeper can improvise, and is praised in the village as “weeping well”.

## 4. Regional and Ethnic Variants

- **The Tujia**: the most developed, with complete suites of bridal lament songs (as “Weeping for Father and Mother”, “Weeping for the Matchmaker”, “Weeping on Mounting the Palanquin”); the “weeping for marriage” may begin ten days or even a month before, with accompanying and answering laments (mother and daughter, sisters, sisters-in-law singing in turn).
- **Han Chinese of Hunan, Hubei, Sichuan and Guizhou**: the lament commonly takes “ten months of pregnancy” as its narrative frame, singing from conception and birth to the wedding.
- **Hakka areas**: called “kujia” or “weeping at the door”, mostly sung when mounting the palanquin.
- **Han Chinese of the north**: mostly loud weeping at the palanquin, without complete sets of verses.
- **Ethnic minorities**: the Miao, Dong, Zhuang, Yao, Yi and Hani all have systematic bridal lament songs, often continuous with “maidens’ songs” and “sisters’ songs”, an important part of the oral literature of each people; their tunes and contents must be recorded separately from those of the Han.

## 5. Objects and Documents

- **No particular objects**: the lament is centred on singing.
- **Handkerchief (the weeping kerchief)**: for wiping tears, often a piece of needlework.
- **Manuscripts of bridal lament songs**: in some regions the oral tradition was written down in the twentieth century, and appears in the collections of folk literature.
- **Recordings and notations**: the form taken by contemporary research into intangible heritage.
