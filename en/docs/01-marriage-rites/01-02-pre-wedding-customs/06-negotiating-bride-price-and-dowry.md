# Negotiating Bride-Price and Dowry

## 1. Definition

The bride-price (also “betrothal money”, “betrothal fee”) and the dowry (also “trousseau”, “portion”) are the two property arrangements agreed by the two sides before a marriage:

- **Bride-price**: property sent by the groom’s family to the bride’s family in accordance with the ritual order and custom, corresponding to nazheng (nabing) among the Six Rites.
- **Dowry**: property prepared by the bride’s family for their daughter, which enters the husband’s household with the bride.

Traditionally the two form a **two-way flow of property**: the groom’s family provides the betrothal gift, the bride’s family prepares the trousseau, and the richness of the trousseau was commonly matched to the bride-price. This structure of counter-flow is the key to understanding the question of the bride-price.

## 2. Historical Origins

- In the ritual classics nazheng is exceedingly light — “dark-and-crimson bolts, paired hides” — and is by nature a symbolic token, not a “purchase price”.
- After the Han and Tang the betrothal goods grew steadily heavier. In Tang and Song statute “betrothal goods” had legal effect: the *Tang Code with Subcommentary* provides that “where the marriage is completed, it ranks as a marriage letter”. The betrothal goods became the material evidence of the engagement.
- In the Ming and Qing “heavy betrothal” was the fashion, and “heavy dowry” with it: to display their standing, the bride’s family continually increased the dowry, producing a climate of “marriage as a question of property”. *Qing bai lei chao* records instances of brides ill-used in the husband’s house because their dowry was meagre.
- In modern times the amount and form of the bride-price have varied with economic conditions, and the difference between town and country is very great.
- Contemporary problems:
  - A high bride-price adds to the burden on families, and in some places has produced “impoverishment through marriage”.
  - Article 1042 of the Civil Code: “Arranged marriages, mercenary marriages and other acts interfering with freedom of marriage are prohibited. The exaction of property by way of marriage is prohibited.”
  - Since 2023 various localities have issued regulations on changing customs and habits, proposing advisory ceilings on the bride-price.
  - Article 5 of Interpretation (I) of the Civil Code, Part on Marriage and the Family, provides for the return of the bride-price in three situations: where the marriage has not been registered; where it has been registered but the parties have not lived together; and where the giving before marriage has left the giver in difficulty.

## 3. Ceremonial Procedure

1. **Negotiating the betrothal**: the matchmaker presides between the two families, settling item by item: the amount of the betrothal money, the number of pieces of jewellery, the quantity of wedding cakes, wine, livestock and cloth.
2. **Writing the list**: when agreed, a gift list (betrothal list) is drawn up, each side keeping a copy.
3. **Negotiating the trousseau**: the bride’s family prepares the dowry according to the scale of the bride-price and its own means, showing or hinting at it to the husband’s family (in some places by “displaying the trousseau”).
4. **Return gifts**: the bride’s family returns part of the gift, the proportion varying from place to place (from a tenth to half).
5. **Delivering the trousseau**: the trousseau is sent before or on the wedding day; see the entry “Preparing and Delivering the Dowry”.
6. **Handling disputes**: if the marriage is broken off, the return of the bride-price is settled by custom and by law; formerly through the elders of the lineage and the village head, now by civil action.

## 4. Regional and Ethnic Variants

- **North China**: the bride-price is chiefly cash, jewellery and cloth, with insistence on the “three golds” (gold ring, gold necklace, gold earrings).
- **Jiangsu and Zhejiang**: the bride-price and the dowry are equally weighted, and the bride’s family often makes a symbolic return of “money pressed into the chest”.
- **Fujian and Guangdong**: the bride-price is high and the dowry heavy, with insistence on “displaying the trousseau”.
- **Parts of Shaanxi and Gansu**: historically the high bride-price has been a conspicuous problem, and in recent years these have been key areas for changing customs.
- **Hakka areas**: the rule is “the man’s family sends pigs, the woman’s family portions out land” (land as the marriage portion).
- **Ethnic minorities**: betrothal gifts take many forms — the Yi reckon them in cattle and silver, the Miao have “betrothal silver”, the Tibetan gift includes tea and butter. Some peoples have systems such as **uxorilocal marriage** and **service marriage** (the man serving the woman’s household for a number of years in place of a bride-price); their logic differs from the Han counter-flow of “bride-price and trousseau” and must be understood on its own terms, without moral judgement.

## 5. Objects and Documents

- **The gift list**: setting out the items and amounts of the betrothal gift.
- **The trousseau inventory**: setting out the items of the dowry.
- **Betrothal money, the three golds, cloth, wedding cakes, wine, livestock**: the contents of the gift.
- **Dowry**: chests, bedding, furniture, household vessels.
- **The reply card**: the document by which the bride’s family acknowledges receipt.
- **Legal documents**: ante-nuptial property agreements (contemporary).
