# Roughhousing in the Nuptial Chamber

## 1. Definition

Roughhousing in the nuptial chamber — also “teasing the chamber”, “making a din in the chamber”, “making a din in the new room” — is the custom of relatives and friends sporting with and teasing the couple in the bridal chamber on the night of the wedding.

Its surface function is “liveliness”, “averting evil” and “breaking the strangeness”; its deeper function is **the social confirmation of the marriage**: through collective participation, a private affair of one household is turned into a public event of the community.

## 2. Historical Origins

- Its early form is “listening at the chamber”: by the Han there was already the custom of relatives and friends listening outside the window on the wedding night.
- The Han record that for three days after a wedding “no music is played, thinking of the continuation of kin”, which stands in contrast to the later music and din of chamber roughhousing, showing that the custom grew gradually after the Han.
- From the Tang and Song the roughhousing flourished. In the Ming and Qing it was universal, with the saying “for three days there is no senior and junior” — within the three days of the wedding, jests might pass regardless of rank.
- In the late Qing and the Republic the roughhousing degenerated in some places into an abuse: lewd language or physical sport with the couple, or quarrels and injuries when the couple refused. The press of the period criticised it frequently. **This is a side that must be stated critically**: roughhousing was meant as blessing, but once it oversteps it becomes an injury to the couple.
- In contemporary weddings the “door-blocking games” and “hunting for the wedding shoe” carry on part of its function, but are generally light and controlled entertainment.

## 3. Ceremonial Procedure

1. **Entering the chamber**: after the couple have entered and sat in the curtain, relatives and friends come in one after another.
2. **Setting the tests**: the company poses difficulties — antiphonal songs, riddles, auspicious talk, set actions to perform.
3. **The couple respond**: the couple answer in concert, and the room cheers.
4. **Singing the chamber songs**: in some regions there are special chamber songs, sung antiphonally by men and women.
5. **Sending the couple to bed**: at an appointed hour the “closer of the round” (such as the *da jin jie* or matron of honour) intervenes and escorts the couple behind the curtain.
6. **Listening at the chamber**: in some places the custom of listening continues, and provides jokes the next day.

## 4. Regional and Ethnic Variants

- **North China**: called “naofang”, with insistence that “the rougher the better”, though chiefly verbal jesting.
- **Jiangsu and Zhejiang**: called “making a din in the new room”, mostly antiphonal songs and riddles, and generally refined.
- **Fujian and Taiwan**: called “roughhousing in the bridal chamber”, the *da jin jie* keeping the measure.
- **Hakka areas**: called “beating the new room”, with antiphonal song contests.
- **The boundary**: wherever the practice involves personal assault, lewdness, forced drinking or humiliation, it has overstepped and is not a legitimate part of traditional custom, but must be clearly resisted.

## 5. Objects and Documents

- **No particular objects**: in chamber roughhousing the people are the “props”.
- **Chamber songs and set verses**: orally transmitted rhymes, written down in some regions.
- **Wedding sweets and fruit**: set out for those who come to roughhouse.
