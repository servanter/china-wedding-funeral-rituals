# Bowing in the Hall (The Three Bows)

![婚礼拜堂场景 / The wedding bowing-in-the-hall ceremony](../../../../assets/images/01-wedding-bowing-hall.png)


## 1. Definition

Bowing in the hall is the kneeling and bowing of the newly married couple; the common “three bows” are: first to Heaven and Earth, second to the “high hall” (the parents), and then the couple bow to one another.

It is the most public and most legally declaratory moment of a wedding: with Heaven and Earth, the ancestors, the kin and the neighbours all present, it proclaims the marriage. **The ritual classics know no “bowing in the hall”, but it discharges the public function of the presentation at the temple and the presentation to the parents-in-law.**

## 2. Historical Origins

- The *Shihunli* has no “bowing in the hall”; its functional equivalents are the “presentation to the parents-in-law” and the “presentation at the temple”.
- The name appears in the Tang and Song. The Song *Dongjing menghua lu*, “Taking a Wife”, records the couple entering the chamber and “bowing to one another”, and the Yuan *Statutes and Precedents* has the words “bowing in the hall”.
- After the Neo-Confucian turn of the Ming and Qing, “bowing to Heaven and Earth” became the highest moment of the wedding, with the symbolic sense that “the wedding is the union of Heaven and Earth”. *Qing bai lei chao*, “Marriage”, records the ceremony in various places.
- In modern new-style weddings bowing replaced kneeling, but the “three bows” structure survived in the “three bows from the waist”.
- Contemporary Chinese-style weddings generally restore the kneeling or the three bows from the waist.

## 3. Ceremonial Procedure

1. **Setting the places**: a “Heaven and Earth table” (or incense table) is set in the hall, with tablets of Heaven and Earth, incense, candles and offerings; the parents sit to either side.
2. **Leading in the couple**: the matron of honour (or the master of ceremonies) leads the couple to the centre of the hall, the man on the left and the woman on the right.
3. **First bow, to Heaven and Earth**: the master cries “first bow to Heaven and Earth”, and the couple kneel towards the table (or bow three times from the waist); this bow establishes the natural and religious ground of the marriage.
4. **Second bow, to the high hall**: the master cries “second bow to the high hall”, and the couple kneel to the parents, who receive the bow (or rise to return it); this bow establishes the marriage’s ground within the lineage.
5. **The couple bow to one another**: the master cries “the bride and groom bow to one another”, and the two bow facing each other; this bow establishes the reciprocal relation of husband and wife. Custom attaches jests to it — “the man bows shallow, the woman deep”, or “whoever raises the head first will rule the house”.
6. **Completion**: the master cries “the ceremony is complete”, firecrackers are set off, and the couple is escorted to the bridal chamber.
7. **Supplementary bows**: in some regions “bowing to the three generations” (to the ancestral tablets) and “bowing to the lineage kin” (greeting the elders of the lineage in turn) are added.

## 4. Regional and Ethnic Variants

- **North China**: the Heaven and Earth table holds a grain measure, a steelyard, a mirror, bow and arrows, incense and candles; at the bowing the incense table is in front and the parents at the side.
- **Jiangsu and Zhejiang**: the order is slightly different — “first Heaven and Earth, then the ancestors, then the parents”.
- **Fujian and Guangdong**: the bowing is held before the ancestral tablets in the main hall, the ancestors first and the parents after.
- **Taiwan**: called “bowing in the hall”, performed before the altar in the main hall, bowing to both the ancestors and the gods.
- **Ethnic minorities**: many peoples have no rite of “bowing to Heaven and Earth”, substituting ceremonies of their own — the Mongolian worship of fire, the Tibetan presentation of *khatas* and offering of wine, the Dai tying of the thread. The function is likewise “to confirm the marriage before the community”, but the religious foundation differs, and the Han “bowing in the hall” should not be treated as equivalent.

## 5. Objects and Documents

- **The Heaven and Earth table or incense table**: the central furnishing of the bowing.
- **Tablets of Heaven and Earth, incense, candles and offerings**: the objects of worship.
- **Red felt and kneeling cushions**: the implements of bowing.
- **Grain measure, steelyard, mirror, bow and arrows**: auspicious objects placed on the table in some regions.
- **The master’s text**: what the master of ceremonies follows.
