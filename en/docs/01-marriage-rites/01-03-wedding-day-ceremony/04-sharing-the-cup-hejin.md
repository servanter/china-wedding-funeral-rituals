# Sharing the Cup (Hejin)

## 1. Definition

Sharing the cup is the rite in which husband and wife drink together. One gourd is split into two ladles, and each takes one and drinks (*yin*, to rinse the mouth with wine or to drink), whence the name. The *jin* was a wine vessel made from a slice of gourd, plain in material, carrying the sense of “sharing one rank”.

The *Liji*, “The Meaning of Marriage”, sums up the beginning of the couple’s life together as “eating from the same victim and drinking from the shared cup”: **eating one animal together (*tonglao*) and drinking from one vessel (*hejin*) is the ritual expression of “union of bodies”.**

## 2. Historical Origins

- The *Liji*, “The Meaning of Marriage”: “When the wife arrives, the husband bows to her to enter; they eat from the same victim and drink from the shared cup, whereby they are united in body and of one rank, and so drawn close.” Kong Yingda’s subcommentary explains further: “one gourd divided into two ladles, husband and wife each holding one ladle to drink”.
- The *Shihunli* records the rite of “tonglao”: the couple share the meat of one tripod, sitting in the chamber, each in a fixed position.
- From the Tang and Song, “hejin” became the “cup of crossed arms”: the Song *Dongjing menghua lu*, “Taking a Wife”, records the couple “joining two cups with a coloured knot and drinking one another’s cup, called the cup of crossed arms”, and that after drinking they threw the cups under the bed and divined from whether they fell face up or face down.
- In the Ming and Qing “hejin” and “crossed cups” were named together, and in popular practice were performed in the bridal chamber.
- Contemporary weddings generally keep the “cup of crossed arms”, the gourd vessels being gone and glasses joined by a red cord used instead.

## 3. Ceremonial Procedure

1. **Eating from the same victim**: the couple sit at one mat and share the meat of one victim (later, one table of dishes), stopping after “three mouthfuls of rice”.
2. **Drinking from the shared cup**: one gourd is divided into two ladles; each holds one and drinks at the same time.
3. **Crossed cups** (Song practice): two cups joined by a coloured knot, husband and wife drinking from one another’s cup — the “cup of crossed arms”.
4. **Divining from the cups** (Song practice): when the drinking is done the cups are thrown under the bed; one face up and one face down is auspicious (“one up, one down — commonly called greatly auspicious”).
5. **Removing the dishes**: the ceremony ends, the wine and food are cleared and the couple retire behind the curtain.
6. **The popular form**: today mostly the “cup of crossed arms” — each holding a cup and drinking with arms linked, with clapping and blessings; in some regions “threading the cup” and the “cup of joined hearts”.

## 4. Regional and Ethnic Variants

- **North China**: called the “cup of crossed arms”, drunk in the bridal chamber with blessings such as “an early noble son”.
- **Jiangsu and Zhejiang**: called the “hejin wine”, with two cups joined by a red cord, the cups kept afterwards as a memento.
- **Fujian and Taiwan**: called the “hejin wine”, usually performed after the bowing in the hall and before entering the chamber.
- **Guangdong**: called “drinking the young-rice wine”, connected with the rite of “shangtou”.
- **Ethnic minorities**: Tibetan weddings include “drinking barley wine together”, the Dai “tying the thread” with mutual toasts, the Mongolian a rite of “offering wine”. The function is likewise “to be joined into one”, but it is generally combined with religious blessing (the chanting of lamas, the tying of a thread at the temple) and should not be treated as the equivalent of Han hejin.

## 5. Objects and Documents

- **The *jin* (half gourd)**: the wine vessel prescribed by the ritual classic, made by splitting a gourd in two.
- **The hejin cups**: the later purpose-made cups, in forms such as the twin cup and the joined cup.
- **Coloured knot and red cord**: for joining the cups (Song practice).
- **Wine**: usually yellow rice wine or rice wine.
- **The food of the shared victim**: the meat and dishes shared.
