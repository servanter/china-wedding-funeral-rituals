# Binding the Hair and Tying the Red Cord

## 1. Definition

Binding the hair means that the newly married couple each take a lock of the other’s hair and tie them together, or knot them with a red cord, symbolising that the two are henceforth one body and will not part in life or in death. From this comes the term “hair-bound husband and wife”.

The ritual background of hair-binding is the coming-of-age ceremony: a man took the cap at twenty and a woman the hairpin at fifteen, both binding up the hair as the mark of adulthood. Marriage then means that two who have come of age “join their hair” into one.

## 2. Historical Origins

- The term appears in the Han. The *Wenxuan* includes Su Wu’s “Four Poems”: “Hair-bound as husband and wife, in love and kindness neither doubting.” It is also used in the *Shiji*, “Biography of Zhufu Yan”.
- In the *Shihunli*, “the mother ties her sash and arranges her kerchief” — the mother tying the sash and kerchief for her marrying daughter — is the ritual archetype within the wedding of the act of “binding”.
- After the Tang “hair-bound” became a byword for the married relation, as in Du Fu’s “Parting on the Wedding Night”: “Hair-bound as wife, the mat not yet warm under you.”
- In the Ming and Qing popular practice it developed into the “rite of hair-binding”: the bride and groom each cut a lock, knotted together with a red cord and placed in a brocade bag or under the pillow.
- The rite of “shangtou” (the marrying woman’s hair changed to the adult coiffure) shares its origin with hair-binding, both involving a change of coiffure; see the entry “Coming-of-Age Coiffure and Face Threading”.
- Contemporary Chinese-style weddings commonly include a “rite of hair-binding” as a performed reconstruction.

## 3. Ceremonial Procedure

1. **Each cuts a lock**: the groom and bride each cut a lock of hair (usually from the temple).
2. **Binding together**: the two locks are knotted into one with a red cord (or red thread).
3. **Blessing**: the master of ceremonies or the woman of full fortune recites a blessing, as “hair-bound as husband and wife, in love and kindness neither doubting” or “white-haired and of one heart”.
4. **Keeping**: the knot is placed in a brocade bag or a scented sachet, or pressed under the new pillow, or tied on the couple’s persons.
5. **Exchange** (in some regions): the bags of bound hair are exchanged as tokens.
6. **The contemporary form**: symbolic locks knotted with red thread, or a red cord tied round the couple’s wrists (“tying the red cord”) — ceremonial in emphasis, the practical meaning largely faded.

## 4. Regional and Ethnic Variants

- **North China**: called “binding the hair” or “joining the hair”, with insistence on “one lock of black hair knotted in one heart”.
- **Jiangsu and Zhejiang**: usually combined with “shangtou”, the woman of full fortune combing and coiling the bride’s hair on the morning of the wedding.
- **Fujian and Taiwan**: called the “rite of binding the hair”, usually performed in the bridal chamber after the bowing in the hall.
- **Guangdong**: named alongside “shangtou”, which is held on the night before the wedding and presided over by the “woman of good fate”.
- **Ethnic minorities**: some peoples have customs of cutting and exchanging hair to express union; the Yi “combing of the hair” and the Yao “coiling of the bun” carry a similar symbolism, but are generally combined with the coming-of-age rite and the fixing of ethnic identity, and their functions are more complex.

## 5. Objects and Documents

- **Scissors**: for cutting the hair.
- **Red cord and red thread**: for knotting.
- **Brocade bag and scented sachet**: for keeping the bound hair.
- **The new pillow and wedding quilt**: where the bound hair is placed.
- **Comb and fine comb**: implements shared with the “shangtou” rite.
