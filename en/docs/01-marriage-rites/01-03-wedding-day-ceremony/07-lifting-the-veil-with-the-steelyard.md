# Lifting the Veil with the Steelyard

## 1. Definition

Lifting the veil is the rite in which the groom raises the bride’s veil with a wedding steelyard (the beam of a balance). *Cheng* (steelyard) sounds like *cheng* “to suit”, giving the sense of “exactly as one would wish”; the beam is marked with stars, giving the blessings “rising step by step” and “stars and measures in plenty”.

It is the most dramatic instant of the wedding: until the veil falls there has been no actual “meeting” for the groom (or for either side); when it falls, the marriage is visually established.

## 2. Historical Origins

- Three accounts are given of the origin of the veil: “to cover shame” (shielding the bride from the gaze of others); “to avert evil” (protecting the bride from baleful influences); and “a vestige of marriage by capture” (a trace of concealment and transfer). All three coexist, the apotropaic account being much the commonest in the old sources.
- By the Eastern Han there was already a custom of the bride “screening her face” (with a fan or cloth); after the Wei and Jin the name “veil” gradually settled, and in Tang and Song weddings the bride screened her face with a veil.
- Ming sources such as *Qing bai lei chao* record the custom of lifting the veil with the steelyard, together with the felicitous sayings recited.
- In the Ming and Qing the lifting might be done by the groom himself, or by the mother-in-law or a woman of full fortune; and there were distinctions between “one lift, two lifts” and even “three lifts”.
- Contemporary Chinese-style weddings generally keep this stage, the steelyard usually replaced by a prop, the ceremony performed rather than lived.

## 3. Ceremonial Procedure

1. **Screening the face**: the bride’s face is covered before she mounts the palanquin (or car) and is not uncovered on the way.
2. **Sitting in the curtain**: on entering the bridal chamber she sits within the canopy, the veil still in place.
3. **Taking up the steelyard**: the groom takes the wedding steelyard (or a *ruyi* sceptre, or chopsticks — *kuaizi* sounding like “a quick son”).
4. **Lifting the veil**: the beam is used to lift the veil from below upwards, in some places with three lifts, accompanied by felicitous sayings:
   - “First lift, heaven and earth enduring; second lift, earth and heaven without end; third lift, sons and daughters in rows.”
   - “The beam lifts the red veil; as one would wish, to white-haired age.”
5. **Cheers**: the company cheers and firecrackers are set off.
6. **Meeting**: the couple see one another, and the following stages — sharing the cup, binding the hair — proceed.

## 4. Regional and Ethnic Variants

- **North China**: the groom lifts the veil with the wedding steelyard, insisting on “three lifts”, with sayings in rhyme.
- **Jiangsu and Zhejiang**: usually lifted by a woman of full fortune or the mother-in-law, or with red silk or a *ruyi* sceptre.
- **Fujian and Taiwan**: called “raising the veil”, lifted by the groom in the bridal chamber, the “sharing of the cup” following.
- **Guangdong**: called “uncovering the head cloth”; in some places it is done as soon as the bride enters, so that she may “bow in the hall and meet the elders”.
- **Ethnic minorities**: most peoples have no “veil”, using head cloths, face veils or shawls for a similar function. Uyghur weddings have a rite of unveiling, presided over by an elder of the groom’s family or an imam with a blessing recited — a ceremony with an Islamic background, whose source differs from the Han idea of the veil as protection against evil.

## 5. Objects and Documents

- **The veil**: usually red silk, embroidered with auspicious motifs, square, with tassels at the edges.
- **The wedding steelyard**: used for the lifting, the beam wrapped in red paper or tied with red cord.
- **The *ruyi* sceptre and chopsticks**: the substitute objects of some regions.
- **The felicitous sayings**: orally transmitted verses, differing from place to place.
