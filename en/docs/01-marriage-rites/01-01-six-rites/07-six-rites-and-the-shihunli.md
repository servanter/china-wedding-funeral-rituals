# Origins of the Six Rites and the Shihunli of the Yili

![六礼仪程图 / The Six Rites flowchart](../../../../assets/diagrams/六礼仪程图.svg)

*Figure 1. The Six Rites — nacai, wenming, naji, nazheng, qingqi and qinying — in procedural order.*

## 1. Definition

This entry examines the textual basis of the Six Rites, the social ranks to which they applied and the history of their interpretation, taking the *Shihunli* (“The Gentleman’s Wedding Rites”) of the *Yili* as its core material. It argues that the Six Rites were not a “written code” but a ritual text recording the wedding procedure of the knightly class, whose formation, naming and interpretation all have historical layers.

## 2. Historical Origins

### The texts

- The *Yili* has seventeen chapters, among which the *Shihunli* is the earliest and most complete record of wedding ceremonial. It sets out the whole sequence — nacai, wenming, naji, nazheng, qingqi, qinying — and appends post-wedding ceremonies such as the bride’s presentation to her parents-in-law, the toasting of the bride and the entertainment of the escort.
- The “Meaning of Marriage” chapter of the *Liji* is the doctrinal exposition: “The wedding ritual is that by which the good of two surnames is joined, so that above one may serve the ancestral temple and below continue the generations.” It also discusses the ethical significance of the Six Rites.
- The “Matchmaker” section of the “Offices of Earth” in the *Zhouli* records the functions of the official matchmaker. This is institutional material that complements the ceremonial level of the *Yili*.

### When the name “Six Rites” was established

- The *Yili* itself nowhere uses “Six Rites” as a collective name for the six procedures; each section is simply called nacai, wenming and so on.
- “Six Rites” as a fixed expression appears in ritual books after the Han — for instance in the discussion of marriage in the *Baihu Tongyi* — and in the commentarial tradition.

### The ranks to which they applied

- The *Shihunli* records the rites of the knightly class (*shi*). The sovereign, the feudal lords and the great officers had regulations of their own: the *Zhouli* records the use of a jade tablet in the sovereign’s betrothal, and the *Liji* records the accompanying-concubine system when a feudal lord married off a daughter.
- Differences of rank under the ritual order showed chiefly in the “number of rites” and in the specification of objects, not in the presence or absence of procedures.

### Principal controversies

1. **Whether nacai and wenming are one rite**: Zheng Xuan’s commentary holds them to be two; some ritual scholars regard them as two stages of a single mission.
2. **How often the wild goose was used**: the ritual classic says “use a goose” for nacai, wenming, naji, qingqi and qinying alike, but since geese were hard to obtain, substitutes were commonly allowed through the dynasties.
3. **The meaning of “driving the bride’s carriage”**: at the personal reception the groom drives the bride’s carriage — one explanation takes this as an expression of affection, another as a survival of ancient custom.
4. **Requesting versus announcing the date**: from the Song onwards ritual books change “requesting the date” to “announcing the date”, a reflection of the husband’s family’s practical initiative.

## 3. Ceremonial Procedure

The overall structure of the Six Rites is as follows (each is treated in its own entry):

| Order | Rite | Performer | Core action | Key objects |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Nacai | Envoy (the guest) | Proposal and acceptance | Wild goose |
| 2 | Wenming | Envoy | Asking name and birth date | Wild goose |
| 3 | Naji | Envoy | Announcing the favourable divination; the match is settled | Wild goose |
| 4 | Nazheng | Envoy | Presenting the betrothal gifts | Dark-and-crimson silks, paired hides |
| 5 | Qingqi | Envoy | Requesting the wedding date | Wild goose |
| 6 | Qinying | The groom | Fetching the bride in person | Black carriage, wild goose, candles |

The first five rites are performed by envoys on the family’s behalf; the sixth is performed by the groom in person. This structural arrangement is itself the meaning of the ritual.

## 4. Regional and Ethnic Variants

Among Han Chinese the Six Rites descended into the customary chain “proposal — horoscope matching — betrothal — sending the date — fetching the bride”. The names of the stages vary enormously from place to place, but the structure broadly corresponds to five segments: negotiation, betrothal, gifts, date and marriage. See the entries under “Han Regional Variants of Marriage Custom”.

## 5. Objects and Documents

- Zheng Xuan’s commentary and Jia Gongyan’s subcommentary to the *Shihunli*: the founding texts of classical interpretation.
- Ritual diagrams and the “wedding vessel-arrangement diagram”: Nie Chongyi’s *Newly Determined Diagrams of the Three Rituals* (Song) and similar works, which provide the basis for a spatial reconstruction of the ceremony.
- Ritual books of the dynasties: the *Great Tang Code of the Kaiyuan Reign*, the *New Ritual Code of the Zhenghe Reign*, Zhu Xi’s *Family Rituals*, the *Collected Rites of the Ming*, the *Comprehensive Rituals of the Great Qing*.
