# Exogamy of the Same Surname and Zhou Marriage Institutions

## 1. Definition

“No marriage between those of the same surname” is the fundamental prohibition of the Zhou marriage system: men and women of the same surname lineage may not intermarry, and the ban is not lifted by distance of generation — “though a hundred generations apart, they may not intermarry”. Connected with this entry are two further elements of the Zhou marriage system: the institution of one principal wife with secondary consorts, and the system of accompanying concubines (*ying*).

## 2. Historical Origins

### The ritual statement of the ban

- The *Liji*, “Great Commentary”: “They are bound by the surname and not distinguished; joined by the sharing of food and not set apart; and though a hundred generations apart, they may not intermarry — such was the way of Zhou.”
- The *Liji*, “Summary of Ritual”: “One does not take a wife of the same surname; hence if one buys a concubine and does not know her surname, one divines it.”
- The *Zuo Commentary*, Duke Xi’s twenty-third year: “Men and women of the same surname — their offspring will not flourish.”

### Three traditions of explanation

1. **Eugenic**: marriage within the same surname fails to multiply offspring (“their offspring will not flourish”); the ancients had already observed the harm of close kin intermarriage.
2. **Lineage exogamy**: marriage with other surnames enlarged the alliance of lineages, the political function of “joining the good of two surnames”.
3. **Ritual distinction of lineages**: those of the same surname and lineage are linked by mourning grades, and intermarriage would confuse the names and statuses of kin; hence it was to be avoided.

Modern anthropology generally explains the ban by exogamy and political alliance together.

### The gap between the ritual order and practice

- Instances of marriage within the same surname are already found in the Spring and Autumn period — Duke Xian of Jin took wives from the Jia and from the Li Ji (Jin and the Li Rong possibly not sharing a surname) — while the *Zuo Commentary* censures many such cases.
- There was thus tension between the prohibition and actual behaviour; the stricter the ban, the more clearly it implies that violations occurred.

### One principal wife with consorts, and the ying system

- In the ritual order “the wife is one only”; consorts were graded (ying, concubines, favourites).
- The ying system: when a feudal lord married off a daughter, a niece (*zhi*) and a younger sister (*di*) accompanied her, together with women from two other states of the same surname — hence “the system of accompanying concubines”.
- Its purpose was to ensure the continuity of the heir and the stability of the marriage alliance.
- The ying system later died out, but the form of one wife with several consorts persisted to the end of the Qing; the *Civil Code*, “Part on Kinship”, of 1930 established monogamy.

## 3. Ceremonial Procedure

The point at which the ban on same-surname marriage was implemented in the wedding procedure was **wenming**:

1. the envoy asks the woman’s “name” (surname);
2. the groom’s family compares it against the lineage genealogy and the origin of the surname;
3. only when it is confirmed that they are not of the same surname and lineage do naji and nazheng follow.

Wenming is therefore not only a question of the birth date but also of the surname: an institutional filter within the Six Rites.

## 4. Regional and Ethnic Variants

- In later ages surname and lineage diverged, and with the bestowal, change and assumption of surnames the surname gradually lost its function of distinguishing lineages; in popular practice “no marriage within the same surname” became **“no marriage within the same lineage”** and **“no marriage within the five degrees of mourning”**.
- Article 1048 of the present Civil Code: “Direct blood relatives and collateral blood relatives within three generations are prohibited from marrying.” A ritual taboo has thus become a legal restriction.
- Some ethnic minorities have different rules: some peoples formerly practised “marriage between the children of a maternal uncle and a paternal aunt” (the uncle’s son taking the aunt’s daughter), which differs from the Han principle of exogamy, being a different structure of kinship, and must be understood on its own terms.

## 5. Objects and Documents

- Genealogies, lineage rules and temple covenants: the basis on which popular practice enforced the bans on same-lineage and within-five-degrees marriage.
- Prohibitory statutes: the “marriage within the same surname” articles of the codes of the dynasties. The *Tang Code with Subcommentary*: “In all cases of marriage within the same surname, each party shall be liable to two years of penal servitude.”
