# Presentation of Betrothal Gifts (Nazheng)

## 1. Definition

Nazheng is the fourth of the Six Rites, also called “nabing” (presenting silks) or “nacheng” (effecting the marriage). The groom’s family sends goods — dark-and-crimson bolts of silk, paired hides and the like — to the bride’s family, and the marriage thereby acquires its substantive constitutive element. “Zheng” means “to complete”; nazheng is “presenting gifts in order to complete the wedding rite”.

The difference between nazheng and naji is that naji need only be “conveyed in words”, whereas nazheng must be “attested by things”. The former confirms an intention, the latter realises a relation. In the ritual order of the pre-Qin period this distinction is perfectly clear.

## 2. Historical Origins

- The *Shihunli*: “Nazheng: dark-and-crimson bolts, paired hides, as in the rite of naji.”
- Zheng Xuan’s commentary: “Zheng means to complete. An envoy is sent to present gifts so as to complete the wedding rite.” Dark (black) and crimson (pale red) are the colours of heaven and earth; five bolts make a bundle; the paired hides are deer skins in pairs, carrying the sense of “in pairs”.
- The *Zhouli*, “Offices of Spring: the Grand Master of Rites”, records that the betrothal gifts of the sovereign and the feudal lords differed in the use of jade tablets and great sceptres — showing that the specification of nazheng matched rank.
- From the Tang and Song onwards the “bolts” gradually became “betrothal money” and “bride-price”, while the ritual books kept the name nazheng. The Song *New Ritual Code of the Zhenghe Reign* and Zhu Xi’s *Family Rituals* both list nazheng (also written nabing).
- The Ming and Qing “guo dali” (major betrothal) is the popularised form of nazheng; the gift list sets out jewellery, cloth, tea cakes, wine and livestock.

## 3. Ceremonial Procedure

1. **Preparation**: the groom’s family prepares dark-and-crimson bolts and paired hides according to the rite (in later practice betrothal money, jewellery, tea, wine and wedding cakes), and writes out the gift list.
2. **Conveyance**: the envoy leads the bearers to the bride’s house, the gift boxes draped in red and accompanied by drums and music.
3. **Setting out the gifts**: on arrival at the central hall the gifts are displayed on tables, and the envoy delivers the formula as at naji.
4. **Receipt and return**: the bride’s family receives the gifts and issues a reply card; part of the gifts is returned (called “huili” or “huipan”), the proportion varying from place to place, and what is returned is often exchanged for things made by the bride’s family.
5. **Feasting**: the bride’s family holds a feast for the envoy and his company.
6. **Announcing to the ancestors**: some lineages announce the marriage to the ancestors, a rite called “gaomiao”.

## 4. Regional and Ethnic Variants

- **North China**: called “guo dali”, “xiapin” or “guoli”; the “fourfold”, “sixfold” or “eightfold” gift is insisted upon.
- **Guangdong**: called “guo dali”; dragon-and-phoenix cakes, coconuts, tea and the three sacrificial meats are indispensable, and everything must be in pairs; the return gift includes “the son-in-law’s trousers” and “the son-in-law’s shoes”.
- **Southern Fujian**: called “songding” or “wanpin”; the gifts are carried in “box panniers”, the numbers reckoned in “pairs”.
- **Hakka areas**: called “zhading” or “guoding”; pork, wine, glutinous rice cakes and cloth predominate.
- **Today**: bride-price and dowry travel in both directions; in some places the excessive weight of the bride-price has given rise to disputes, a negative aspect of the modernisation of wedding custom that must be stated critically. Article 1042 of the present Civil Code expressly “prohibits the exaction of property by way of marriage”.

## 5. Objects and Documents

- **Dark-and-crimson bolts**: three bolts of dark (black) and two of crimson (pale red) made a bundle; these later became betrothal money and silk.
- **Paired hides**: deer skins in pairs, carrying the sense of pairing.
- **The gift list or betrothal letter**: the document setting out the items and quantities of the betrothal gift.
- **Dragon-and-phoenix cakes, wedding cakes, tea cakes**: betrothal gifts common in later practice.
- **The reply card**: the document by which the bride’s family acknowledges receipt.
