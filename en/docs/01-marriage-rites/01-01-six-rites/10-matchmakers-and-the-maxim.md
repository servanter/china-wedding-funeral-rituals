# Matchmakers and the Maxim “Parents' Command, the Go-Between's Word”

## 1. Definition

*Meishuo* means the matchmaker. The *Shuowen jiezi*: “*Mei* is to plan, to plan the joining of two surnames.” “*Shuo* is to weigh, to weigh the two surnames one against the other.” The institution of the matchmaker is the social mechanism by which the “command of the parents” was realised as marriage: without the mediation of a matchmaker, negotiation and engagement could not be ritually valid.

“The command of the parents, the words of the go-between” comes from the *Mencius*, “Duke Teng Wen, Part II”; its converse is that “if one does not wait for the command of the parents and the words of the go-between, but bores a hole and peeps, climbs over the wall and follows, then parents and people alike despise them”. The go-between is thus not merely an introducer but the guarantor of the marriage’s legitimacy.

## 2. Historical Origins

### The pre-Qin period: from custom to office

- The *Shijing*, “Qi Odes: South Mountain”: “How does one take a wife? One must tell one’s parents.” “How does one take a wife? Without a matchmaker one cannot.” This shows that by the Spring and Autumn period the matchmaker was already a general institution.
- The *Zhouli*, “Offices of Earth: the Matchmaker”: “The Matchmaker has charge of the pairing of the myriad people; for all men and women from the giving of a name upwards, he records the year, month, day and name.” And: “For all who take a divorced wife or bring in a child, he records it.” This is the beginning of the **official matchmaker**: the office kept the register of marriages among the people and held the mid-spring assembly (“in the month of mid-spring, he causes men and women to meet”).
- The first five of the Six Rites are performed by an “envoy (*bin*)”, and that envoy is a matchmaker under commission: the go-between and the ritual procedure are one.

### Han and Tang: from office to profession

- After the Han the functions of the official matchmaker contracted and private matchmakers arose, the office being most often filled by middle-aged and older women.
- In the Tang the marriage letter and engagement card had to bear the matchmaker’s signature, and the “matchmaker” became a necessary element of the marriage contract as witness.

### Song, Yuan, Ming and Qing: a trade of the marketplace

- The Song *Dongjing menghua lu*, “Taking a Wife”, and *Mengliang lu*, “Marriages”, record in detail the matchmaker’s shuttle diplomacy: negotiating, carrying cards and bringing the parties together.
- In the Yuan and Ming there was a system of “official matchmakers” with charge of the remarriage of widows, the official sale of persons and the marrying off of convicted women — the matchmaker taking on a quasi-administrative function.
- In the Ming and Qing matchmaking became a trade, with a distinction between “arranging a match” and “guaranteeing a match”, and popular customs of “three teas and six rites” and “thanking the matchmaker”.

### Modern times

- The matchmaker has gradually given way to the marriage agency and the dating platform, the function shifting from “ritual guarantee” to “information broker”.
- In the negotiation of the bride-price and in communication between the two households, however, the matchmaker still has a practical role.

## 3. Ceremonial Procedure

The chain of the matchmaker’s functions in a marriage:

1. **Proposal**: conveying a message to the bride’s family on the groom’s behalf, or seeking a husband for a daughter on the bride’s family’s behalf.
2. **Negotiation**: carrying the conditions of each side back and forth (family standing, age, land, the amount of the betrothal gift).
3. **Carrying cards**: exchanging the horoscope cards and assisting in the match-reading.
4. **Negotiating the bride-price**: settling the betrothal gift and the dowry, acting as broker.
5. **Sending the date**: conveying the date slip and coordinating the wedding date.
6. **Reception**: attending on the day of the reception, mediating in customs such as “blocking the gate” and “opening the face”.
7. **Thanking the matchmaker**: when the marriage is accomplished, both families give gifts in thanks.

## 4. Regional and Ethnic Variants

- **The north**: called “meipo” or “introducer”; the thank-offering is usually a pig’s head, shoes and socks, and a red packet.
- **Jiangsu and Zhejiang**: politely called “the Old Man of the Moon” or “the Crimson Maiden”; the thank-offering includes “the matchmaker’s shoes” and “the matchmaker’s pig’s trotters”.
- **Fujian and Guangdong**: called “meiren po” or “da jin jie”; the *da jin jie* presides over the ceremonies on the wedding day, combining the office of master of ceremonies.
- **Ethnic minorities**: among the Zhuang, “song takes the place of a matchmaker, and the match is fixed by antiphonal singing”; among the Miao, Yi and Dong the maternal uncle or the village elder often acts as matchmaker; some peoples practise “the maternal uncle presiding over the marriage”, the function of the go-between being discharged directly by kinship.
- Although the matchmaker was a requirement of the ritual order, with the spread of free courtship the office is no longer indispensable, and its surviving forms appear chiefly in the negotiation of the bride-price and in the conduct of ceremonies.

## 5. Objects and Documents

- **The matchmaker’s attestation**: the matchmaker’s name on the marriage letter and engagement card, constituting a witness in the sense of popular law.
- **The matchmaker’s card**: an intermediary document recording the particulars of both parties and the agreed conditions.
- **Thank-offerings**: a pig’s head (“the matchmaker’s pig”), shoes and socks, cloth and red packets, varying from place to place.
- **The official matchmaker’s seal**: the official voucher used after the Yuan by official matchmakers in handling remarriages and arranged unions.
