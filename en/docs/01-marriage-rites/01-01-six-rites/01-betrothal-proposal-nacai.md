# Betrothal Proposal (Nacai)

## 1. Definition

Nacai is the first of the Six Rites recorded in the *Shihunli* (“The Gentleman’s Wedding Rites”) of the *Yili*. The groom’s family sends a matchmaker (the envoy) to the bride’s family to declare its wish to marry, presenting a wild goose as the introductory gift (*zhi*).

“Cai” means to select; “nacai” is therefore “presenting the gift of selection” — the groom’s family having already fixed on this woman, it now formally seeks the bride’s family’s consent. Of the Six Rites, nacai is the only exploratory step: the bride’s family may politely decline at this point, and the remaining five rites follow only after nacai has been accepted.

## 2. Historical Origins

- The *Shihunli* opens: “The wedding rite: the proposal is sent down; nacai, with a wild goose.” Zheng Xuan comments: “Wishing to join in marriage with that family, one must first send a matchmaker to convey the words; when the bride’s family consents, one then sends a man to present the gift of selection.”
- The *Liji*, “The Meaning of Marriage”: “The wedding ritual is that by which the good of two surnames is joined, so that above one may serve the ancestral temple and below continue the generations.” The subcommentaries explain nacai as the rite of “selection”, the beginning of “reverence, care, gravity and correctness”.
- Three explanations are given for the use of the goose: geese move with the yang and so accord with yin and yang; geese keep order in flight and at rest — flying in rows, settling in ranks — a figure of the good order of the wedding; and a goose pairs for life and takes no second mate, a figure of conjugal fidelity. All three appear in the ritual commentaries.
- From the Han onwards nacai and wenming were increasingly combined, sometimes simply called “the declaration”. Sima Guang’s *Book of Ceremonies* and Zhu Xi’s *Family Rituals* reduced the Six Rites to three — nacai, nabing (presenting silks) and qinying — folding the negotiation of the marriage and the asking of the name into nacai.
- Because geese were hard to obtain, substitutes were common through the dynasties. The *Comprehensive Rituals of the Great Qing* allowed that “where there is no goose, a gander may replace it”; in popular practice geese, chickens and carved wooden geese were used, or sugar, wine and cakes instead.

## 3. Ceremonial Procedure

1. **Preliminary conveyance**: the groom’s family first sends a matchmaker to express its wish to the bride’s family; only when the bride’s family consents may nacai proceed.
2. **Preparation of the gift**: the father of the groom instructs the envoy (*bin*) to take up the goose and set out in ceremonial dress (*xuanduan*).
3. **Receiving the guest**: the envoy arrives outside the bride’s gate; the host (the bride’s father) comes out to meet him outside the gate, and they bow and yield precedence as they enter.
4. **Ascending the hall**: host and guest ascend the hall, and the guest, holding the goose, delivers the formula: “You have been so gracious as to bestow your daughter upon so-and-so of our house. So-and-so has the rites of his forebears, and has sent me to request nacai.” (In substance: since you are willing to give your daughter to a man of our house, we have prepared the gift of our ancestors and sent me to perform nacai.)
5. **The reply**: the host answers: “My daughter is dull and stupid and I have been unable to teach her; since you command it, I dare not refuse.” (Humbly calling his daughter foolish, he does not presume to decline.) This is the fixed humble formula prescribed by the ritual books, and is highly stereotyped.
6. **Presentation of the goose**: the guest presents the goose; the host receives it, turns and hands it to an attendant.
7. **Entertaining the guest**: the host sets out sweet wine (or wine and food) in the guest’s honour; the guest withdraws, and the host escorts him beyond the gate and bows twice.

## 4. Regional and Ethnic Variants

- **North China (Beijing, Tianjin, Hebei, Shanxi)**: called “tiqin” or “shuo xifu”; the matchmaker calls with wine, sweets and cakes, and if the woman’s family accepts the gift and answers, the marriage is agreed.
- **Jiangsu and Zhejiang**: called “timei” or “yigin”; stress is laid on matching family standing, and the matchmaker is often a relative of one or both families.
- **Fujian and Guangdong**: called “shuoqin”; a live chicken or wedding cakes often serve as the gift. In Chaoshan a proposal requires the “fourfold gift” of sugar, cakes, tea and wine.
- **Ethnic minorities in the south-west**: among the Miao, courtship through the “youfang” and “tiaoyue” gatherings precedes the sending of a matchmaker; the Yi have a formal “shuomei” etiquette in which the matchmaker must call three times before the match is settled.
- In today’s popular practice “tiqin” and “kan jiamen” carry on the sense of nacai, the goose having been largely replaced by chickens, geese, wine, sugar and cakes.

## 5. Objects and Documents

- **The goose** (or a gander, chicken or carved wooden goose): the central introductory gift, carrying the senses of accord with yin and yang, good order, and constancy.
- **The proposal card (*tiqin tie*) or gift card (*zhi tie*)**: a popular document conveying the proposal and the groom’s family’s particulars.
- ***Xuanduan* ceremonial dress**: the robe worn by the envoy, the ceremonial dress of the knightly class.
- **The matchmaker’s fee**: the later gratuity to the go-between; not part of the ritual classic, but a popular addition.
