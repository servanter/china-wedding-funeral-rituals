# Personal Reception of the Bride (Qinying)

## 1. Definition

Qinying is the last of the Six Rites and the only one performed by the groom in person. At dusk on the wedding day the groom, in ceremonial dress and riding a black carriage, goes himself to the bride’s house to receive her, lays the goose on the hall floor, and brings her home.

The *Liji*, “Duke Ai’s Questions”, records Confucius: “When the great wedding comes, he dons the cap and receives her in person, doing her honour.” And again: “A wife is the mistress of the intimate sphere; how dare one fail in respect towards her?” Qinying is thus invested with the ethical sense of “honouring the wife”, and is the most humanly coloured section of the Zhou marriage system.

## 2. Historical Origins

- The *Shihunli* sets out the ceremony in detail: “At the beginning of the dusk of the appointed day, three tripods are set outside the chamber door… The head of the house wears the *juebian* cap, a crimson skirt with a black border; his attendants are all in *xuanduan*; he rides a black carriage, with two following carriages, candle-bearers before the horses.”
- The *Shijing*, “Greater Odes: Great Brightness”: “Wen determined its auspiciousness, and received her in person at the Wei.” This is the reception by King Wen of Zhou, held up by later ages as the model.
- The *Spring and Autumn Annals* censures the failure to receive in person — as in “Ji Liexu came to escort the woman” under Duke Yin’s second year, which the Gongyang tradition reads as a criticism of the ruler’s failure to go himself. Qinying was thus already, in the Spring and Autumn period, an important standard for judging whether a marriage was ritually correct.
- In later ages the rule was applied flexibly: commoners and knights usually received in person, while officials, distant households or special circumstances might send a son or an envoy instead — “yingqu”, “jieqin”. From the Tang and Song the rite merged with the custom of fetching the bride in a bridal palanquin.
- In modern times “jieqin” and “yingqin” carry on the name, though the ceremony has been much simplified.

## 3. Ceremonial Procedure

1. **Setting out**: at dusk on the wedding day the groom’s house lays out food and prepares the carriage and horses, with candles set as a guide ahead, and the groom in the *juebian* cap, crimson skirt and black border.
2. **Arrival at the bride’s house**: the groom arrives in his carriage outside the bride’s gate; the host comes out to meet him, and they bow and yield precedence as they ascend the hall.
3. **Laying the goose**: on the hall the groom lays down the goose (or presents it to the host), then descends the steps and bows to the ground twice.
4. **Admonishing the daughter**: the bride’s parents admonish their daughter in her chamber; her mother ties her sash and arranges her kerchief, charging her not to disobey her parents-in-law.
5. **Leaving the chamber**: the bride comes out of her chamber with attendants; the groom meets her and bows to her to precede him.
6. **Returning together**: the groom himself drives the bride’s carriage for her, or they ride in separate carriages, returning with the train; at the gate of the groom’s house he enters first, and when she arrives bows to her to enter.
7. **Entering the chamber**: inside, the rites of “hejin” (sharing the cup) and “tonglao” (sharing the meat) are performed; see the entries “Hejin” and “Wedding-Day Ceremonies”.

## 4. Regional and Ethnic Variants

- **North China**: the groom rides in a bridal palanquin or on horseback, at the head of a reception party with drums and wind music, the rule being “go odd, return even”.
- **The water towns of Jiangsu and Zhejiang**: the bride is fetched in a “reception boat”, its prow hung with red, commonly called the “flower boat”.
- **Fujian and Guangdong**: formerly a bridal palanquin, now usually a decorated car; in Chaoshan there is a custom of “sacrificing to the ancestors before the reception”.
- **Ethnic minorities**: Miao receptions include “gate-blocking wine” and antiphonal singing; the Yi have “carrying the bride on the back”; the Zhuang “fetch the bride with antiphonal song”; the Dai have the custom of “tying the thread”.
- Some peoples formerly practised “not taking up residence in the husband’s house” or “walking marriage”, and their reception ceremonies differ widely from the Han; see the entries for each people.

## 5. Objects and Documents

- **The black carriage**: the pre-Qin carriage of the knightly reception, its body painted black, in the sense of plainness.
- **The bridal palanquin**: the chief vehicle of later receptions, borne by four, eight or even sixteen bearers.
- **Candles**: the ritual classic’s “candle-bearers before the horses”, a survival of the wedding being held at dusk.
- **Wedding dress**: the *juebian* cap and crimson skirt in the pre-Qin period; later the phoenix crown and rosy cape, and the *qun kwa*.
- **The goose**: the gift laid down at the personal reception.
- **The decorated car, and bands of gongs, drums and *suona***: the modern forms.
