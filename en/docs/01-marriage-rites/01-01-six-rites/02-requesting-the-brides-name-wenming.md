# Requesting the Bride’s Name (Wenming)

## 1. Definition

Wenming is the second of the Six Rites. When nacai has been completed, the envoy, still holding the goose, asks the name and the birth date and hour of the woman, so that the groom’s family may divine the auspiciousness of the match in the ancestral temple. Because the “name” asked for was to be settled by divination, the ritual books also call this rite “asking by divination”.

Three readings are current in ritual scholarship as to what “name” means: the woman’s personal name; her surname (in order to observe the ban on marrying one’s own surname); or both the name and the date of birth. From the *Yili*’s wording — “I venture to ask whose daughter she is” — the identification of the surname is plainly part of the matter.

## 2. Historical Origins

- The *Shihunli*: “The guest, holding the goose, asks the name; the host consents, the guest enters, presents it, as in the first rite.”
- The subcommentary to the “Meaning of Marriage” in the *Liji*: “The asking of the name is in order to divine its good or ill fortune” — making the purpose of wenming explicit.
- Zheng Xuan’s commentary: what is asked is “the woman’s name”, which is taken home and divined in the temple; if the omen is favourable, naji follows.
- From the Tang and Song onwards wenming was generally combined with nacai. Wu Zimu’s *Mengliang lu* (“Marriages”) and Meng Yuanlao’s *Dongjing menghua lu* (“Taking a Wife”) record that after the two parties had seen one another “cards were at once exchanged” — the popular reduction of wenming.
- In Ming and Qing popular practice the exchange of horoscope cards and the matching of the eight birth characters took over the function of wenming; the ritual sense of the rite disappeared, and “whether the two sets of birth characters agree” became the central concern.

## 3. Ceremonial Procedure

1. **Asking the name**: when nacai is done, the guest, holding the goose, says: “So-and-so has received your command and will lay the matter before the divination; I venture to ask whose daughter she is.” (The consent to marry having been given, the omen is to be taken; may I ask the young lady’s name and birth date.)
2. **The reply**: the host answers: “You have given your command, and would choose her to fill a place among your number; I dare not refuse.” He then states the woman’s surname, personal name, order among her siblings, and the year, month, day and hour of her birth.
3. **Presentation of the goose**: the guest presents the goose and the host receives it, as in nacai.
4. **Divination on return**: the envoy returns to the groom’s house, and the head of the household divines upon the name and birth date in the family temple (the later “divination by stalks” or “match-reading”).
5. **Decision**: if the omen is favourable, naji is performed; if not, the marriage is dropped.

## 4. Regional and Ethnic Variants

- **Exchange of horoscope cards (dragon-and-phoenix cards)**: the most widespread form. Each family writes on red paper the names of three generations, the birth characters of the person concerned, native place and order of birth; the cards are exchanged through the matchmaker — “exchanging cards”, “exchanging horoscopes”.
- **Division of cards**: in Shanxi and Hebei a “rough card” and a “fine card” are distinguished — the rough card first, by way of sounding out, the fine card afterwards, to settle the matter.
- **Fujian and Taiwan**: called “songgeng” or “dui bazi”; the card is written in regular script with a brush on red paper, and corrections and erasures are strictly avoided.
- **Match-reading (*hehun*)**: judgement by the stems and branches of the birth characters, the nayin pitches and the mutual attraction or clash of the zodiac animals; this belongs to the culture of date selection, and is treated in the entry “Horoscope Matching”.
- **Ethnic minorities**: the Manchu and Mongolian peoples formerly had shamans or lamas divine the match; Tibetan marriage also attends to the compatibility of zodiac animal and birth year.

## 5. Objects and Documents

- **The goose**: the gift prescribed by the ritual classic.
- **The horoscope card (dragon-and-phoenix card)**: the physical vehicle of wenming in popular practice — red paper folded in two, the man’s card marked “qianzao” (the male destiny) and the woman’s “kunzao” (the female destiny).
- **Rough and fine cards**: the two stages of wenming and engagement in the Song dynasty (see *Dongjing menghua lu*, “Taking a Wife”).
- **Instruments of divination**: tortoise shell and yarrow (ritual) or the tube of lots, copper coins and the casting of the eight characters (popular practice).
