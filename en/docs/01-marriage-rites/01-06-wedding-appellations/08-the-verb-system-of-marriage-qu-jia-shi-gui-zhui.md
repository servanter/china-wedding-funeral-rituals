# The Verb System of Marriage (Qu, Jia, Shi, Gui, Zhui)

## 1. Definition

The group of verbs denoting the conclusion of a marriage, carrying **direction, hierarchy and lineage nuance**.

| Verb | Use | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Qu (to take a wife)** | the man’s family takes a wife | the sense of “taking”; the man’s family acts |
| **Jia (to give a daughter in marriage)** | the woman’s family gives a daughter | the sense of “going”; the woman goes to a man |
| **Shi** | a woman’s marriage | the *Erya*: “a daughter goes to a man” |
| **Gui (“to go home”)** | a woman’s marriage | the *Shijing*: “this girl goes home”; *gui* means going to the husband’s house |
| **Zhui (to marry into the wife’s family)** | the man enters the woman’s household | also “coming to the door”, “calling in a son-in-law”, “the door hung the other way” |
| **Zhao (calling in a son-in-law)** | the woman’s family calls in a son-in-law | “calling in a *zhui*”, “calling in a man” |
| **Pin (to take a wife with betrothal gifts)** | engaging by sending the betrothal gift | “taken by *pin* is a wife” |
| **Na / naqie (to take a concubine)** | taking a secondary consort | distinguished from “buying a concubine” |
| **Zi** | a woman’s promise in marriage | “awaiting the character in the women’s quarters” |
| **Zaijiao / gaijia (to remarry)** | a widow’s remarriage (old usage) | *jiao* was an ancient wedding drinking rite |
| **Xuxian / tianfang (to remarry, of a man)** | a man taking another wife | literary and popular usage |

**Difference of meaning**: “qu” and “jia” mark a one-directional form of marriage in which **the man’s family acts and the woman’s family is acted upon**; “zhui” and “zhao” mark the contrary and exceptional form, and hence carried pejorative overtones in the traditional context (the “*zhui* son-in-law” having low standing).

## 2. Historical Origins

- **Pre-Qin**: the *Erya*, “Explaining Kin”, and the *Yili*, “Mourning Attire”, systematically use *shi*, *jia* and *gui*.
- **Han and Wei**: the *Shiming*, “Explaining Kinship”, explains *jia* by sound and meaning: “a woman going to a man is *jia*”.
- **Tang and Song onwards**: “qu” and “jia” are the ordinary terms; the customs of “marrying in” and “calling in a son-in-law” appear in the codes and gazetteers.
- **Ming and Qing**: the codes laid down rules of inheritance for the *zhui* son-in-law and the called-in son-in-law.
- **Today**: “marrying” and “setting up a household” are the ordinary terms; the talk of “marrying in” fades, and new forms appear such as “the marriage of two houses” and “the two-ended marriage”.

## 3. Ceremonial Procedure

1. **Pin**: the man’s family sends the betrothal gift and the engagement is made.
2. **Zi**: the woman’s family promises the marriage.
3. **Jia / qu**: on the wedding day the woman’s family “gives the daughter” and the man’s family “takes the wife”.
4. **Yugui**: the new wife enters the husband’s household.
5. **Zhui / zhao (the exception)**: the man enters the woman’s household, changing his surname or not according to the agreement and the lineage rules.

## 4. Regional and Ethnic Variants

- **Han regions**: “qu” and “jia” are general; marrying in is comparatively common in the southern mountains (as in southern Zhejiang and northern Fujian).
- **Jiangnan**: the “marrying-in son-in-law” is called “the son-in-law who comes to the door”, the ceremonies simplified.
- **Ethnic minorities**: some peoples practise “uxorilocal residence” (as with the Dai and some Yi branches), the direction of the marriage being the reverse of the Han; these must be recorded separately.

## 5. Objects and Documents

- **Documents**: marriage letters (mostly using *qu* and *shi*), contracts of marrying in (documents of uxorilocal marriage).
- **Textual basis**: the *Erya*, “Explaining Kin”; the *Shiming*; the *Chengwei lu*.
