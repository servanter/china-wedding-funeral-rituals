# Affinal Kin Terms

## 1. Definition

| Term | Relation | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **ZhoulI (sisters-in-law, wives of brothers)** | between the wives of brothers | *zhou* and *li* are the mutual terms of brothers’ wives |
| **Lianjin (“linked lapels”, co-brothers-in-law)** | between the husbands of sisters | *jin* means lapels joined; in the north also “one carrying-pole” |
| **Gusao** | between the husband’s sisters and the wife | *gu* means the husband’s sisters (“elder *gu*”, “younger *gu*”) |
| **Wengxu (father- and son-in-law)** | between the wife’s father and the son-in-law | *weng* means the father-in-law |
| **Shusao, xiongsao** | between the husband’s brothers and the wife | “elder brother-in-law”, “younger brother-in-law” |
| **Shan (elder sister’s husband and younger brother’s wife)** | classical | rare today |
| **Jiusao** | the wife of the wife’s brother | generally “jiusao”, “elder *jiu*”, “younger *jiu*” |
| **Biao kin (maternal and paternal aunts’ lines)** | cousins derived from affinal links | “paternal-aunt cousins”, “maternal-aunt cousins” |

**Ritual significance**: affinal terms of the same generation mark the duty of **cooperation among peers** (sisters-in-law running the household together, co-brothers-in-law helping one another), complementing the order within the lineage.

## 2. Historical Origins

- **Pre-Qin**: the *Erya*, “Explaining Kin”, calls sisters-in-law “*dìfu*” and “*sifu*” (the elder brother’s wife *si*, the younger brother’s wife *dì*).
- **Han and Wei**: the *Shiming* explains the sense of “*disi*”.
- **Tang and Song onwards**: “zhoulI” and “lianjin” gradually became the ordinary terms.
- **Ming and Qing**: the *Chengwei lu* lists the local variants in detail (as “one carrying-pole”, “linked lapels”).
- **Today**: the countryside still uses “zhoulI” and “lianjin”; the cities mostly generalise to “sister-in-law” and “brother-in-law”.

## 3. Ceremonial Procedure

1. **The wedding day**: the couple greet the same-generation affines of both households, and the terms are established.
2. **Acknowledging kin**: visits are paid household by household, gifts exchanged.
3. **Everyday dealings**: sisters-in-law manage the household together, co-brothers-in-law assist one another.
4. **Seasonal gatherings**: they meet at the festivals, the terms now fixed.
5. **After household division**: the sisters-in-law’s relation turns to dealings between separate households.

## 4. Regional and Ethnic Variants

- **North China**: co-brothers-in-law are called “one carrying-pole” or “linked lapels”.
- **Jiangnan**: sisters-in-law are called “*shubomu*” or “zhoulI”; co-brothers-in-law “lianjin”.
- **Fujian and Taiwan**: sisters-in-law are “*tongsi*”, co-brothers-in-law “*tongmen*”.
- **Guangdong**: co-brothers-in-law are called “lianjin” or “old lapel”.
- **Ethnic minorities**: the terms for same-generation affines answer to each people’s own system (as with peoples having “preferential cross-cousin marriage”, where the terms are bound up with the rules of matching); these must be recorded separately.

## 5. Objects and Documents

- **Documents**: marriage letters, gift lists, genealogies (the descent tables record sisters-in-law relations).
- **Textual basis**: the *Erya*, “Explaining Kin”; the *Shiming*; the *Chengwei lu*.
