# Marriage-Related Kinship Terms: An Overview

## 1. Definition

Marriage terms divide into the three stages **“before the wedding — at the wedding — after the wedding”** and four systems:

| System | Content | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Intermediary terms** | The matchmaker and go-between roles | Old Man of the Moon, Crimson Maiden, ice man, matchmaker woman |
| **Terms at the wedding** | Groom and bride and related roles | groom, bride, “official man”, “lady”, bridesmaid, matron of honour |
| **Affinal kin terms** | Kin created by the marriage | the in-laws (*qingjia*), parents-in-law on either side, sisters-in-law, co-brothers-in-law |
| **Marriageable age and verbs** | Stages of marriageable age and verbs of marrying | hairpin-taking, cap-taking, “awaiting betrothal”, “leaving the chamber”; to take a wife, to be given in marriage, to marry (of a woman), to go home (to the husband’s house), to marry into the wife’s family |

**The core change**: after marriage the terms **rise in rank** — “aunt” becomes “mother-in-law” (the husband’s mother), “aunt” becomes “mother-in-law” (the wife’s mother); at the same time the terms used by the wife’s own family take up the standpoint of “the outer house” (as in “daughter”, “aunt-in-chief”). A change of term is a change of **status and obligations** (the counterpart, in the mourning grades, of the five-degrees system).

## 2. Historical Origins

- **Pre-Qin**: the *Erya*, “Explaining Kin”, systematically arranged kinship terms — the earliest monograph on them; the *Yili*, “Mourning Attire”, defined closeness and distance through mourning grades.
- **Han**: the *Shiming*, “Explaining Kinship”, explained the origin of terms by sound and meaning.
- **Tang and Song onwards**: marriage terms ran alongside popular sayings; literary terms such as “Crimson Maiden” and “Old Man of the Moon” spread from Tang romances and Yuan drama.
- **Qing**: Liang Zhangju’s *Chengwei lu* gathered them together comprehensively.
- **Today**: spoken terms are simplified (“uncle” and “aunt” generalised), while written and ritual usage survives in wedding and funeral ceremonies.

## 3. Ceremonial Procedure

1. **The negotiation**: the matchmaker travels between the houses, each addressing the other with honorifics such as “your good daughter” and “your good son”.
2. **After engagement**: “fiancée” and “fiancé”; the two sets of parents address one another as “in-laws” (*qingjia*).
3. **The wedding day**: “the new couple (groom and bride)”; the change of address to “father, mother” (the “rite of changing address”).
4. **After the wedding**: affinal terms are established, and the “acknowledging kin” rites are performed according to the relation.
5. **After children are born**: the terms rise in rank again (“their father”, “their mother”; within the lineage, terms formed from the child’s standpoint).

## 4. Regional and Ethnic Variants

- **North China**: the usual forms are formed from the child’s standpoint, as “his father”, “his mother”.
- **Jiangnan**: dialectal terms are rich — “*apo*”, “*asao*”, “*niangjiu*” (mother’s brother).
- **Fujian and Taiwan**: terms often preserve old sounds and old senses (“hand-in-hand”, “the great one”, “new wife”).
- **Ethnic minorities**: each people’s kinship terms answer to its own kinship system (as with the Yi lineage-branch terms and the Mosuo matrilineal terms); the Han system does not apply, and they must be recorded separately.

## 5. Objects and Documents

- **Textual basis**: the *Erya*, “Explaining Kin”; the *Shiming*; the *Chengwei lu*.
- **Documents**: marriage letters, wedding cards, gift lists, genealogies (with their terms of descent).
- **Common honorifics**: “your honoured father”, “your honoured mother”, “your good daughter”, “your good son”, “honoured in-laws”.
