# Terms of Legitimate and Secondary Status, Remarriage and Concubinage

## 1. Definition

Terms of status within the traditional marriage system, directly bound up with **lineage succession**.

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Principal wife (*zhengshi*, legitimate, first-married)** | the wife formally taken in marriage |
| **Successor wife (*jishi*, “filling the chamber”, “joining the string”)** | the wife taken after the first wife’s death |
| **Secondary consort (*ceshi*, concubine, “lady of like rank”, “aunt”)** | a spouse other than the principal wife |
| **Equal wife (the two wives of an heir-adopting marriage)** | particular historical circumstances (as with a marriage serving two lines) |
| **Son by the wife (*dizi*, legitimate)** | son of the principal wife |
| **Son by a concubine (*shuzi*, secondary)** | son of a concubine |
| **Legitimate mother / secondary mother (birth mother)** | the legitimate son calls the principal wife his legitimate mother and the concubine who bore him his secondary or birth mother |
| **Outer consort (*waishi*)** | an informal spouse not brought into the household |

**Ritual significance**: the distinction of legitimate and secondary directly affected **lineage succession, mourning grades (as with the mourning of a secondary son who succeeds his father) and family standing**; it is a core category of the lineage system.

## 2. Historical Origins

- **Pre-Qin**: the lineage system established “succession by the eldest legitimate son”, and the terms of legitimate and secondary were already systematic in the ritual classics and histories.
- **Han and Tang**: the statutes laid down the status of legitimate and secondary; special cases of “serving two lines” and the “equal wife” appear in case records.
- **Ming and Qing**: the codes (*Great Ming Code*, *Great Qing Code*) set out in detail the status of wife and concubine and legitimate and secondary succession.
- **Modern times**: the *Civil Code*, “Part on Kinship”, of 1931 established monogamy, and concubinage was abolished in law.
- **Today**: only monogamy exists in law, and the terms of legitimate and secondary are historical vocabulary (found in literature and genealogies).

## 3. Ceremonial Procedure

1. **Negotiation and marriage**: the principal wife was taken through the Six Rites; the taking of a concubine generally did not use them.
2. **The wedding ceremonies**: the principal wife performed the “bowing in the hall” and the “sharing of the cup”; a concubine mostly only “paid her respects”.
3. **Presentation to the parents-in-law**: the new wife greeted her parents-in-law, establishing her standing as wife.
4. **Entry in the genealogy**: the principal wife is entered as “took a wife of such-and-such a surname”; a concubine is mostly recorded as “secondary consort of such-and-such a surname” or not entered at all.
5. **Sacrifice and mourning**: legitimate and secondary differed in mourning grades and in the right to sacrifice (as with the mourning of an adopted heir).

## 4. Regional and Ethnic Variants

- **Han regions**: the legitimate-secondary distinction was strictest in lineage society (Fujian and Guangdong, Huizhou).
- **Ethnic minorities**: some peoples historically had polygyny or polyandry, with different terms and institutions, not belonging to the Han legitimate-secondary system; these must be recorded separately.

## 5. Objects and Documents

- **Documents**: marriage letters (principal wife), genealogies (with descent and legitimate-secondary notations), case records of the codes.
- **Textual basis**: the *Erya*, “Explaining Kin”; the *Chengwei lu*; the *Great Ming Code*; the *Great Qing Code*.
