# Honorific and Self-Deprecating Terms

## 1. Definition

In dealings among kin and in wedding/funeral documents, one uses an **honorific** (*zuncheng*) for the other party's relatives and a **self-deprecating term** (*qiancheng*) for one's own — the courtesy of "honouring others, humbling oneself".

| Object | Honorific | Self-deprecating |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Father | Your honoured father (*lingzun*, *zungong*) | my father (*jiafu*, *jiayan*) |
| Mother | Your honoured mother (*lingtang*, *zunci*) | my mother (*jiamu*, *jiaci*) |
| Elder brother | Your honoured brother (*lingxiong*) | my brother (*jiaxiong*) |
| Son | Your honoured son (*linglang*, *lingsi*) | my boy (*xiaocong*, *quanzi*) |
| Daughter | Your honoured daughter (*ling'ai*, *lingyuan*) | my daughter (*xiaonü*) |
| Wife | Your honoured wife (*zunfuren*, *saofuren*) | my wife (*zhuojing*, *neiren*) |
| Son-in-law | Your honoured son-in-law (*lingxu*, *dongchuang*) | my son-in-law (*xiaoxu*) |
| Deceased elder | Your late honoured father (*lingxianzun*) | my late father (*xianfu*, *xianci*) |

**"Jia for the elder, She for the younger, Ling for the other's"** is the three basic rules: for one's own elders use "jia" (my father, my brother); for one's own juniors use "she" (my younger brother, my nephew); for the other party's relatives use "ling" (your father, your son).

## 2. Historical Origins

- The *Liji* "Qu Li" already shows the honorific/humble distinction of "calling oneself so-and-so" and "calling the other's son".
- Han–Wei letter manuals (the *Yueyi*, *Shuyi*) set the family-letter term format.
- From the Tang and Song the "ling/jia/she" system took shape in correspondence.
- The Ming–Qing daily-use encyclopaedias (the *Complete Letter-Writer* type) widely record the various terms of wedding/funeral documents.
- Today spoken honorifics and humble forms have faded, but written ones (obituaries, invitations) must still be observed.

## 3. Ceremonial Procedure

1. **Sort the party**: "ling" for the other's relatives, "jia" for one's own elders, "she" for one's own juniors.
2. **Sort living/dead**: the deceased get "xian" (late father, late mother); the living get "jia".
3. **Enter documents**: the terms and self-references of invitations, obituaries and elegies follow the layout.
4. **Sign off**: when writing letters or sending gift lists, sign with a self-reference (e.g., "affinal younger brother", "kinsman nephew").
5. **Spoken address**: face-to-face address still mainly uses the kinship term; honorific/humble forms are chiefly written.

## 4. Regional and Ethnic Variants

- **North China**: spoken honorifics such as "nin" (you, honoured) and "the old one" (*ta laorenjia*).
- **Jiangnan**: the written honorific/humble system is best preserved.
- **Fujian, Guangdong**: spoken reduplicative nicknames (a-dad, a-mum); written still uses honorific/humble forms.
- **Ethnic minorities**: each has its own language's honorific system; the correspondence with the Chinese honorific/humble system must be recorded separately.

## 5. Objects and Documents

- **Documents**: invitations, marriage contracts, obituaries, elegies, letters.
- **Objects**: letter paper, name cards.
