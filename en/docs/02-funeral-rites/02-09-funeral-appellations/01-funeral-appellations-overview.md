# Overview of Funeral Appellations

## 1. Definition

Funeral appellations are **the whole set of names used in a funeral context**, marked by the parallel of "**taboo**" (*hui*, not naming directly) and "**respect**" (*jing*): the dead are called by respectful or tabooed terms, the bereaved by humble terms, the guests by polite terms. They are classified as follows:

| Class | Examples | Entry |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Terms for the deceased** | Xiankao, Xianbi, Xiankao, late wife | 02 |
| **Mourning master and filial kin** | Mourning master, filial son, filial daughter, filial daughter-in-law | 03 |
| **Mourning self-appellations** | Gu zi, Ai zi, Gu Ai zi, unfilial son | 04, 05 |
| **Tabooed terms of death** | Beng, Hong, Zu, Bulu, passed away | 06 |
| **Institutional appellations** | Dingyou, shouzhi, duoqing, qifu | 07 |
| **Name-and-title class** | Posthumous name, temple name, spirit-lord name | 08 |
| **Functionary appellations** | Great guest, rite-master, master of ceremonies, bearers | 09 |
| **Condolence terms** | Diao, Yan, Fu, Zeng, Sui | 10 |

**Two basic principles:**

1. **"Taboo"** — do not call the dead by name directly, but by taboo-name, style, birth-order, or office (*hui mou*, *zi mou*).
2. **"Marking near/far"** — the wording of an appellation directly marks the degree of mourning (e.g. "qi-fu grandson", "xiao-gong nephew"), matching the five-degree system.

## 2. Historical Origins

- **Pre-Qin**: the *Erya* ("Shiqin") is the earliest systematic work on kin terms; the *Liji* ("Quli") fixes the graded taboo-terms "beng, hong, zu, bulu, si".
- **Han-Tang**: name-taboo (*bihui*) was strict; funeral appellations were closely tied to taboo; official funerals had granted-office and posthumous-name terms.
- **Song-Ming**: the *Family Rituals* standardises the obituary and lord-inscribing appellations; the *Chengwei Lu* (Qing, by Liang Zhangju) gathers them all.
- **Ming-Qing**: the obituary's "mourning-degree line" format matured and fixed.
- **Modern**: funeral appellations survive in obituaries, elegiac couplets and inscriptions; contemporary obituaries still use "Xiankao", "unfilial son", "weeping blood and bowing the head to the ground".

## 3. Ceremonial Procedure

1. **First death**: rename the dead "hui mou", "Xiankao / Xianbi"; the living call themselves "unfilial son".
2. **Obituary**: phrase the obituary by the appellations, listing the mourning-degree line.
3. **Inscribe the lord, make the banner**: write "Xiankao so-and-so, taboo so-and-so, the spirit-lord".
4. **Answer condolences**: answer with self-appellations like "weeping blood and bowing the head to the ground", "wiping tears and bowing the head".
5. **Inscription and spirit-lord**: the appellations are finally fixed, used long-term.

## 4. Regional and Ethnic Variants

- **Han, all regions**: the appellations are roughly alike, with dialectal popular names (e.g. "the old one", "the forebear").
- **Min-Tai (Fujian-Taiwan)**: obituaries and elegiac hangings keep more old-style appellations; "Xiankao" and "Xianbi" run in parallel.
- **Ethnic minorities**: each people's kin-term system relates to its own kinship system (e.g. the Yi clan terms), not applying the Han funeral-appellation rules, and must be recorded separately.

## 5. Objects and Documents

- **Literary basis**: *Erya* "Shiqin", *Liji* "Quli", *Chengwei Lu*.
- **Document carriers**: obituary, elegiac statement, elegiac couplet, hanging, spirit-lord, epitaph, inscription.
- **Common formulas**: "weeping blood and bowing the head to the ground", "zhang-qi", "qi-fu", "gong-fu", "si-fu".
