# Gu Zi, Ai Zi, Gu Ai Zi

## 1. Definition

The mourner's self-appellation changes by **the combination of the parents' life and death**, the wording rule for the obituary's signature:

| Self-appellation | Case | Source and note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Gu zi** (orphan son) | Father dead (mother alive) | *Liji* "Quli": "the orphan son in the chamber, his cap and clothes not of pure colour" |
| **Ai zi** (grieving son) | Mother dead (father alive) | Takes the sense of "grief" |
| **Gu Ai zi** | Both parents dead | "gu" + "ai", the heaviest self-appellation |
| **Ji ren** (thorn-man) | A general term for the mourner | *Shijing* "Kuai feng", "Su Guan": "the thorn-man, lean and gaunt" |
| **Unfilial son** | A humble term for the bereaved's son | Common (modern obituaries mostly use it) |

**Usage points**:

- Male mourning masters mostly use "gu zi / ai zi / gu ai zi"; females use "gu nü / ai nü".
- The obituary's end often reads "**Gu zi so-and-so, weeping blood and bowing the head to the ground**".
- "Gu" in the pre-Qin also meant "a son who lost his father"; combined with "ai" it became a funeral-specific term.

## 2. Historical Origins

- **Pre-Qin**: the *Liji* ("Quli", "Zaji") already has "gu zi"; the *Shijing* "thorn-man" is the mourner's term.
- **Tang-Song**: the "gu zi / ai zi" distinction settled in obituaries and letters.
- **Ming-Qing**: the obituary format matured, signing "gu zi / ai zi / gu ai zi" by the parents' life and death, with "weeping blood and bowing the head to the ground" etc.
- **Modern**: old-style obituaries keep it; contemporary obituaries mostly use "unfilial son / daughter", sometimes "gu ai zi".

## 3. Ceremonial Procedure

1. **First death**: the bereaved's son changes to gu zi / ai zi / gu ai zi.
2. **Make the obituary**: sign by the rule, ending with "weeping blood and bowing the head to the ground".
3. **Receive condolences and answer**: the mourning master performs and thanks by the self-appellation.
4. **Spirit-lord, inscription**: used with "Xiankao", "Xianbi" etc.
5. **Remove the dress**: after the term, the self-appellation returns to normal.

## 4. Regional and Ethnic Variants

- **Han, all regions**: old-style obituaries keep it; folk speech uses "filial son" loosely.
- **Min-Tai (Fujian-Taiwan)**: obituaries and thanks-notes use "gu ai zi", "weeping blood and bowing the head to the ground".
- **Ethnic minorities**: mourning self-appellations mostly follow the people's own vocabulary and taboos (not naming one's own name), not the same system as the Han "gu zi / ai zi", and must be recorded separately.

## 5. Objects and Documents

- **Carriers**: obituary, elegiac statement, thanks-note, spirit-lord, tombstone.
- **Formulas**: "weeping blood and bowing the head to the ground", "wiping tears and bowing the head", "wiping tears and knocking the head" (by the mourner's near/far).
- **Literature**: *Liji* "Quli", *Chengwei Lu*.
