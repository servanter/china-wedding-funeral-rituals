# The Mourning Basin and the Weeping Staff

## 1. Definition

The **mourning basin** (*sangpen*, also "old basin", "filial basin", "earthen basin") is the earthen basin the mourning family uses to burn paper money and sacrifice; the **weeping staff** (*kusangbang*, also "filial staff", "mourning staff", "grief staff", "leaning staff") is the staff the filial son (and certain kin) holds in the mourning period. Both are **mourning implements** by which the filial son shows his ruin and supports his body.

- **Weeping staff (mourning staff)**: the *Liji* ("Wensang"): "the filial son mourning kin weeps without count, serves diligently three years, his body ill and weak, and leans on the staff for his illness" — the staff's original meaning is the filial son's grief and weakness, needing the staff to lean on.
- **Mourning basin**: folk uses it to burn paper money, and derives the custom of "**smashing the basin**" (at raising the coffin, dashing the basin to the ground).

**The basin-smashing rite**: at raising the coffin, the eldest son (the succession-bearer) lifts the basin high and dashes it; folk see it as a symbol of "smashing away care" and "inheriting the estate", with the saying "the more shattered the better". The smasher is mostly the **succession-grandson or eldest son**, tied to lineage succession.

## 2. Historical Origins

- **Pre-Qin**: the *Liji* ("Wensang") and *Yili* ("Sangfu") record the staff system — the staff differs in bamboo and tong (zhan cui uses the strung staff of bamboo; zi cui uses the cut staff of tong).
- **Han-Tang-Song-Ming**: the staff follows the ritual, with clear grades; the *Family Rituals* fixes the filial son's staff-holding.
- **Ming-Qing**: folk "weeping staff" simplifies; mostly willow, bamboo or sorghum stalk, wrapped in white paper.
- **Modern**: the mourning-basin and basin-smashing custom is especially strong in the north; the basin's form and the smashing time differ by place.

## 3. Ceremonial Procedure

1. **Make the staff**: the filial son makes the staff (bamboo, tong or willow), the length mostly to the chest, wrapped in white paper or plain cloth.
2. **Hold the staff**: after assuming the dress, the filial son holds the staff, leaning on it in coming and going, kneeling, sending the coffin.
3. **Burn**: in the mourning period burn paper money in the mourning basin.
4. **Smash the basin to raise the coffin**: at raising the coffin, the succession-bearer smashes the basin; after it breaks, the coffin sets forth.
5. **Set the staff**: some areas set the staff by the grave after burial, or burn it at the "third-day return".

## 4. Regional and Ethnic Variants

- **North China**: the basin-smashing rite is strongest; the smasher must be the eldest son or succession-grandson; the basin is earthen (commonly "old basin").
- **Jiangnan**: the filial son holds the staff in mourning, but basin-smashing is less common than the north, mostly named "burning the paper basin".
- **Min-Tai (Fujian-Taiwan)**: the "filial staff" differs from the "hemp staff"; the filial son holds the staff, the end wrapped in white cloth.
- **Some southwestern areas**: the filial son holds a bamboo staff, stressing the number of bamboo nodes.
- **Ethnic minorities**: some peoples mark the filial son's status with a staff or stick, whose meaning (e.g. exorcism, leading the way) differs from the Han "leaning on illness", and must be recorded separately.

## 5. Objects and Documents

- **Mourning basin**: earthen, pottery basin ("old basin"), some with a hole in the bottom.
- **Weeping staff (staff)**: bamboo staff (strung), tong staff (cut), willow staff; wrapped in white paper or cloth.
- **Burning implements**: money-burning furnace, paper money.
- **Ritual**: the staff's material and use follow the five-degree grade, matching the mourning-dress system.
