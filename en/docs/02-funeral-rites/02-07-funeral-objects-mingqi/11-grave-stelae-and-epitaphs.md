# Grave Stelae, Epitaphs and Spirit-Way Stelae

## 1. Definition

The three are all carved-stone texts, differing in use and position:

| Term | Position | Content | Style |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Grave stele** (tomb tablet) | Erected on the surface before the grave | The dead's name, birth-death, descendants, the stele-raiser | Narrative |
| **Epitaph** (grave inscription) | Buried in the grave (near the coffin) | The "zhi" (prose life-account) + the "ming" (rhymed elegy) | Epitaph form |
| **Spirit-way stele** | Erected by the spirit-way before the grave | Office, deeds, granted posthumous name | Stele-biography form |

**Form**: the grave stele is mostly a vertical rectangular stone, with a base (tortoise) below and a cap (dragon-head) above; the epitaph is two squares, the "zhi cover" (title) and the "zhi stone" (text), placed together in the grave.

**Ritual meaning**: "**setting up a stele and making a biography**" — the stele is the carrier by which the dead "transmit their name to later ages"; its style has been highly mature since the Han-Wei, and is the first-hand material for studying family, office and geography.

## 2. Historical Origins

- **Han**: steles and upright stones rose; the Eastern Han grave stele peaked, "steles springing up like clouds"; the grave stone-carving (stone figures, stone beasts, spirit-way stele) was a matched system.
- **Wei-Jin, Northern and Southern Dynasties**: steles were once banned, so the custom of **burying the epitaph in the grave** flourished; the Northern Wei epitaph is especially fine (e.g. the Yuan-family epitaphs).
- **Tang**: steles and epitaphs especially flourished; the spirit-way stele and epitaph became important literati styles (Han Yu, Liu Zongyuan have famous pieces).
- **Song-Yuan-Ming-Qing**: the system continued; the *Family Rituals* rules the form of the grave stele and epitaph; Ming-Qing official tombs had spirit-way steles and stone figures.
- **Modern**: the grave stele simplifies to an upright stone; the epitaph is rare; contemporary cemeteries mostly carve name and birth-death, sometimes placing an "epitaph" with the ashes casket.

## 3. Ceremonial Procedure

1. **Write the text**: invite a notable or literatus to write the epitaph and stele text (mostly on a life-account and a brief biography).
2. **Write and carve the stone**: invite a calligrapher to write, a carver to engrave.
3. **Erect the stele**: mostly erect the stele on a chosen day after burial (the "stele-erecting rite"), kin and friends sacrificing.
4. **Bury the epitaph**: at burial place the epitaph before the coffin (or in the grave door), covered by the zhi cover.
5. **Later upkeep**: genealogies, gazetteers and epigraphy works record the stele-epitaph text.

## 4. Regional and Ethnic Variants

- **North China**: tall steles, mostly with stone pillars and stone beasts, stressing the cap carving.
- **Jiangnan**: the stele calligraphy is stressed; Ming-Qing literati stele-epitaph art is high.
- **Min-Tai (Fujian-Taiwan)**: the grave-stele text stresses "hall name" and "ancestral seat" (e.g. "the Yingchuan Chen clan"); the stele-cap carves the place or hall name.
- **Ethnic minorities**: some peoples have a tradition of standing and carving stone (e.g. the Tibetan mani stone, some peoples' stone-mound grave markers); their cultural thread differs from the Han stele-epitaph, and must be recorded separately.

## 5. Objects and Documents

- **Stele stone**: bluestone, white marble, granite; base, cap, stele pavilion.
- **Epitaph**: zhi cover, zhi stone (square, mostly with a domed cover).
- **Documents**: life-account, brief biography, tomb table, spirit-way stele text, epitaph; the genealogy's "biography" and "grave map".
- **Records**: epigraphy works like *Jinshi Cuibian* and *Yushi*.
