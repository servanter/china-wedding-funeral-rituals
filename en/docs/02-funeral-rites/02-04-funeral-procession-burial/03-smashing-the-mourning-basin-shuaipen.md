# Smashing the Mourning Basin (Shuaipen)

## 1. Definition

Smashing the basin (*shuaipen* — also *shuaisang pen*, *shuai wa pen*, *shuai lao pen*) is the custom, at the raising of the coffin, of the chief mourner (or the heir-bearing grandson) lifting above the head and dashing to the ground the earthen basin used for burning paper before the spirit.

Its surface function is the "signal to send off the coffin" — once the basin breaks, the procession sets forth; its deeper function is a **public declaration of inheritance**: in North China and elsewhere, the basin-smasher is the one who "smashes the basin and holds the banner", publicly recognised as the principal heir to the estate. Hence in old times childless men would "adopt" a nephew to "smash the basin", directly tying the rite to inheritance and succession.

## 2. Historical Origins

- Smashing the basin is unrecorded in the ritual classics and belongs to the folk level. Its origin may have three sources:
  1. It relates to the ancient custom of "destroying vessels": breaking an object to express "the living and the dead are forever parted";
  2. It relates to the use of the brazier: paper is burned before the spirit, the ash gathered in an earthen basin, and when the coffin leaves the basin is no longer used, so it is smashed to "send it away";
  3. It relates to the inheritance system: smashing the basin, as a public act of "receiving the succession", was acknowledged by the lineage.
- Ming and Qing gazetteers and notebooks record "smashing the basin" abundantly, alongside the saying "he who smashes the basin divides the family property".
- Every place had clear rules on who smashes: if there is a son, the eldest son; if no son but an adopted heir, the heir; if no heir, a nephew or adopted son (often causing disputes).
- In some areas the shards of the smashed basin were swept up after the procession and buried in a set spot, not left to be trampled.
- Under the contemporary urban cremation system the basin-smashing survives as a ritual link (especially in the north), but is no longer directly bound to inheritance.

## 3. Ceremonial Procedure

1. **Prepare the basin**: set an earthen basin (the "mourning basin") before the spirit, used for burning paper during the vigil, filled with paper ash.
2. **Choose the person**: fix the smasher (eldest son / heir-bearing grandson / adopted heir).
3. **Place the basin**: before raising the coffin, set the basin before the chief mourner (or at a designated spot outside the door), filled with paper ash.
4. **Kneel and pray**: the smasher kneels before the basin, kowtows to the coffin, and speaks a prayer.
5. **Smash the basin**: lift the basin above the head (or level), and dash it to the ground with force, breaking it in one blow (if it does not break, do not smash again — have another break it, or it is inauspicious).
6. **Raise the coffin**: once broken, raise the coffin and set forth, with loud wailing.
7. **Sweep the shards**: afterwards sweep up the shards and bury them outside the door or by the grave.
8. **Confirm the status**: under the old system the smasher was acknowledged as the "succession heir" and recorded in the lineage.

## 4. Regional and Ethnic Variants

- **North China**: the custom is strongest; "smashing the old basin" is one of the most critical acts of the funeral, paired with "holding the banner" as "smashing the basin and holding the banner".
- **Northeast**: mostly the eldest son smashes, insisting on "smashing it through to the end".
- **Jiangnan**: some areas do not smash a basin but a "bowl" or "jar", or replace it with "burning the basin" (burning basin and ash together).
- **Fujian-Guangdong**: mostly "burning the paper basin", with little basin-smashing.
- **Contemporary**: rural north and some urban funerals keep it as a ritual symbol; its legal link to inheritance no longer holds (the Civil Code's succession chapter takes statutory and testamentary succession as standard).
- **Ethnic minorities**: smashing the basin is a Han custom (especially northern), and most other peoples lack it. The Yi "offering the animal and dividing the meat" and the Miao "dividing the ox" relate to inheritance in form, but belong to ethnic customary law and are not the same mechanism as smashing the basin.

## 5. Objects and Documents

- **Mourning basin (earthen basin)**: the core object, mostly coarse earthenware.
- **Paper ash**: what the basin holds.
- **Filial staff, spirit banner**: objects paired with smashing the basin ("smashing the basin and holding the banner").
- **Lineage register, household-division document**: documents once tied to smashing the basin (now without legal effect).
