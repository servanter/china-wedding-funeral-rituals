# Returning the Spirit and Acknowledging Thanks

## 1. Definition

- **Returning the spirit** (*huiling*): after burial, the family brings the "spirit seat" (or soul-silk) home and sets it up, called "returning the spirit".
- **Installing the spirit** (*anling*): setting the spirit seat in the hall and continuing to serve it until the mourning is removed (or until the hundredth day, the anniversary).
- **Acknowledging thanks** (*xiexiao*): after the funeral is done, the family thanks the kin, neighbours and functionaries who took part, called "acknowledging thanks".

Burial is not the end of the funeral: **after "burial" comes "sacrifice"**. Returning the spirit is precisely the transition that brings the sacrifice from before the grave back into the home.

## 2. Historical Origins

- The *Yili* ("Jixi li") records the "returning wail" (*fanku*) after burial: the procession returns to the ancestral temple and wails — the earliest ritual form of returning the spirit.
- The *Liji* ("Wensang"): "send the form away, and welcome the essence back" — the form is sent to the grave, the spirit (soul) welcomed home; this is the meaning of returning the spirit.
- Zhu Xi's *Family Rituals* prescribes "returning wail at the temple" after burial, and sets a spirit seat and performs the "repose sacrifice" (*yuji*, the first formal sacrifice after burial, three in all). **After the repose sacrifice, the "offering" is renamed "sacrifice".**
- Ming and Qing gazetteers record the customs of "returning the spirit" and "installing the spirit"; some places serve the spirit seat at home to the hundredth day, some to the three-year removal of mourning.
- "Acknowledging thanks" is much recorded in Ming and Qing gazetteers, e.g. "when the funeral is done, the filial son calls to acknowledge thanks at the door", "on the day of acknowledging thanks, the mourners receive the filial son's bow".
- In contemporary cities the funeral is mostly done at the funeral home; "returning the spirit" is simplified to setting a portrait and incense table at home, served to the hundredth day or anniversary.

## 3. Ceremonial Procedure

### Returning and installing the spirit

1. **Returning wail**: after burial, the procession returns (first to the shrine or home) and wails.
2. **Repose sacrifice**: on the day of burial set the "repose sacrifice" (first repose), the first formal sacrifice after burial; then the "second" and "third repose". After the repose sacrifice, the funeral's "offering" turns into "sacrifice".
3. **Welcome the spirit seat**: welcome the spirit seat (or soul-silk, portrait) home.
4. **Install the spirit**: set the spirit seat in the hall (or a special "spirit-seat table"), with incense, candles and offerings.
5. **Morning and evening sacrifice**: until the mourning is removed, still offer morning and evening incense and food (lighter than during lying in state).
6. **Choose a date to remove the spirit**: choose a day (e.g. hundredth day, anniversary, three years) for the "removing the spirit" and "removing the mourning" rite (see "Removing Mourning and the Dan Sacrifice").

### Acknowledging thanks

1. **Choose the day**: after the funeral, choose a day to acknowledge thanks (mostly within three to seven days after burial, or after the third-day return).
2. **Prepare gifts**: prepare thanks-gifts (cakes, sweets, towels, wine), or hold a "thanks note".
3. **Call at the door**: the filial son calls in turn to bow thanks: first the lineage elders and the funeral managers (the director, the accountant, the ritual master); then the mourners and gift-givers; then the neighbours and helpers; separately the roadside offerers and procession-followers.
4. **Perform the rite**: the filial son arrives, kneels or bows to thank; the thanked mostly returns the rite and comforts.
5. **Post the thanks note**: some places post a "thanks note" on their own door, thanking all sides.
6. **Thanks feast**: some areas hold a "thanks feast" for the helpers and functionaries.
7. **End**: when thanks are done, the funeral's social exchanges return to normal (some intercourse limits still hold before the mourning is removed).

## 4. Regional and Ethnic Variants

- **North China**: acknowledging thanks mostly after the third-day return; the filial son calls and kneels, the thanked "raises him up and will not accept".
- **Jiangnan**: called "acknowledging thanks" and "thanking guests", mostly within seven days after burial.
- **Min-Tai (Fujian-Taiwan)**: called "acknowledging thanks" and "thanking guests", with the further "removing mourning" rite (see "Removing Mourning and the Dan Sacrifice").
- **Guangdong**: called "acknowledging thanks", mostly done successively after the "seven-day" rites.
- **Ethnic minorities**: some peoples have their own "thanking guests" rite after burial — the Yi "rewarding guests", the Miao "thanking the village"; the Tibetan thank by inviting monks to chant and dedicate merit; Muslim peoples thank those who attended the funeral but have no rite named "acknowledging thanks". The concrete form must follow each people's custom.

## 5. Objects and Documents

- **Spirit seat, soul-silk, portrait**: the objects of returning and installing the spirit.
- **Spirit-seat table, incense, candles, offerings**.
- **Thanks note**: the acknowledgement document (format in "Document Templates / Thanks-Note and Acknowledgement Model").
- **Thanks-gifts**: cakes, sweets, towels, wine.
- **Ritual book**: the basis for calling door by door.
