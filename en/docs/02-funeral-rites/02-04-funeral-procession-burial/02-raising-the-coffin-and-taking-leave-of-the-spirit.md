# Raising the Coffin and Taking Leave of the Spirit

## 1. Definition

- **Raising the coffin** (*qiling*, also *qiling*, *fayin*): the rite of lifting the coffin from the mourning hall, moving it out of the door, and formally setting out.
- **Taking leave of the spirit** (*ciling*): the family's final farewell before the coffin, also called "taking leave", "farewell", "sacrificing to the spirit".

Raising the coffin is the moment of transition from stillness to motion in the funeral: the vigil of the preceding days is a static companionship, and after the coffin is raised everything begins to move toward the grave. Hence the loudest weeping comes at this moment, in the saying "once the coffin is raised, the wailing moves heaven".

## 2. Historical Origins

- The *Yili* ("Jixi li") records in detail the rites before the procession ("the evening weeping" *jixi ku* is the "evening weeping" of the previous day):
  - **Moving the coffin** (*qianjiu*): moving the coffin from the mortuary to the ancestral temple ("greeting the ancestors" *chaozu*), the "temple-visiting" rite;
  - **Farewell offering** (*zudian*): an offering at the ancestral temple, announcing the impending burial;
  - **Dispatch offering** (*qianmian*): the offering on the day of the procession, the most solemn.
- Zhu Xi's *Family Rituals* simplifies the procedure but keeps "moving the coffin to greet the ancestors", the "dispatch offering" and "starting the procession".
- The names "raising the coffin" and "taking leave of the spirit" became current in the Ming and Qing, recorded in many gazetteers.
- In contemporary earth-burial areas "raising the coffin" is still performed; in urban funeral homes it is joined with the "body-farewell ceremony".

## 3. Ceremonial Procedure

1. **Taking leave of the spirit (farewell)**: on the night before or the morning of the procession, family members in turn go to the spirit, perform the kneeling-and-bowing rite, and weep their farewell.
2. **Withdraw the offering**: remove the vessels of the morning and evening offering, set the "dispatch offering" (the procession offering), with richer provisions.
3. **Read the eulogy**: read the final sacrificial text ("the text of the send-off feast").
4. **Move the coffin (greet the ancestors)**: carry the coffin to the ancestral temple (or shrine) to report to the ancestors, called "greeting the ancestors"; those without a shrine report toward the ancestral grave from outside the gate.
5. **Seal the coffin**: check the lid and the coffin canopy, tighten the ropes (if the coffin is not yet nailed, nail it now).
6. **Raise the coffin**: the pall-bearers lift the coffin; the chief mourner leads the way holding the "wailing staff" (*kusang bang*, the filial staff).
7. **Fire crackers and raise the lament**: crackers are fired, kin wail, the coffin goes out the door.
8. **Taboos of leaving the door**: the coffin must not strike the door-frame ("striking the door" is inauspicious); in some places the coffin must go "feet forward, head behind" (head outward is "leaving the door"; the exact direction varies by place and must be recorded separately, not stated uniformly).
9. **Smash the basin**: at the door the basin-smashing rite is performed (see the entry "Smashing the Mourning Basin").
10. **Set forth**: the procession sets out in order.

## 4. Regional and Ethnic Variants

- **North China**: called "raising the coffin" and "procession", emphasising "the chief mourner leads with the staff" and "setting forth after smashing the basin".
- **Jiangnan**: called "starting the procession" and "raising the coffin", with a clear distinction between "greeting the ancestors" and "taking leave of the spirit".
- **Min-Tai (Fujian-Taiwan)**: called "going to the mountain" and "procession", the coffin carried by *tugong*, with a Daoist priest chanting in the lead.
- **Guangdong**: called "procession" and "going up the mountain", linking "taking leave", "raising the coffin" and "smashing the basin" in sequence.
- **Ethnic minorities**: some peoples' "raising the coffin" is accompanied by special rites and songs — the Yi "pointing the road" (*zhilu*) sings the "road-pointing scripture" at the procession, guiding the soul to the land of the ancestors; the Miao "opening the road" (*kailu*) is led by a shaman; Tibetan sky-burial is carried by the "corpse-bearer" to the sky-burial platform. These are not "raising the coffin" but independent rites with a soul-guiding function.

## 5. Objects and Documents

- **Coffin, coffin canopy, carrying poles, ropes**: implements of raising the coffin.
- **Wailing staff (filial staff)**: the chief mourner holds it to lead the way.
- **Dispatch-offering provisions, eulogy**.
- **Firecrackers**: the signal to raise the coffin.
- **Guiding banner, road-buying money**: set out with the procession.
- **Rooster (some areas)**: to press the coffin and dissolve evil.
