# Roadside Offerings (Luji)

## 1. Definition

The roadside offering (*luji*) is the rite by which, as the procession passes a thoroughfare, street corner or the residence of kin and friends, those kin and friends set up an incense table with offerings by the road and block the way to sacrifice.

The feature of the roadside offering is "taking the road as the altar": no venue is required, only an incense table and a few offerings, and the sacrifice can be made. It extends the funeral from "the home" into "public space", letting more neighbours and fellow tradesmen who cannot easily come to the mourning hall pay their respects. Thus the roadside offering is also an important expression of the funeral's social character.

## 2. Historical Origins

- The *Yili* ("Jixi li") records an offering (*dian*) on the way to the burial, but does not detail the name "roadside offering".
- The Han had the "sacrificing to the road" (*zudao*, sacrificing to the road god before a journey), which is genealogically linked to the funeral "roadside offering".
- From the Tang and Song the "roadside offering" was current; the Song *Dongjing Menghua Lu* records the customs of "roadside offering" and "blocking-the-road offering" on the way to the burial.
- The Ming and Qing saw it at its height; gazetteers record "kin and friends sacrifice by the road, and the family must descend and bow in thanks" and "great households have as many as dozens of roadside offerings".
- The roadside offering also had a "social-pressure" side: the number and scale of offerings measured the family's social standing, breeding extravagance, so through the ages there were orders "forbidding roadside offerings" (e.g. the Ming once forbade excessive folk roadside offerings).
- Contemporary cities have largely abandoned roadside offerings; villages still keep the surviving customs of "crossing offering" and "blocking-the-road offering".

## 3. Ceremonial Procedure

1. **Agree the offering**: kin and friends tell the family in advance where they wish to make the roadside offering, and the family agrees (some are set up on the spot).
2. **Set the table**: by the road set an incense table with incense, candles, fruit, wine and paper money, hang a mourning couplet.
3. **Wait for the column**: the offerer waits by the road for the procession.
4. **Block and sacrifice**: as the column arrives, the offerer steps forward to block and sacrifice, lighting incense and kneeling.
5. **Return the rite**: the chief mourner (filial son) descends (or stops) and kneels to thank, the bereaved accompanying in weeping bows.
6. **Burn paper**: the offerer burns paper money and fires crackers.
7. **Proceed again**: the rite done, the column continues.
8. **Record**: the number of roadside offerings and the offerers are recorded afterward in the ritual book, for the thanks-gifts.

## 4. Regional and Ethnic Variants

- **North China**: called "roadside offering" and "blocking-the-road offering", insisting "the filial son descends and kneels in thanks".
- **Jiangnan**: called "roadside offering", mostly at street corners and bridgeheads.
- **Min-Tai (Fujian-Taiwan)**: called "roadside offering", linked with "seeing off guests"; the procession reaches a spot where the seeing-off guests take leave.
- **Guangdong**: called "roadside offering", mostly at the village entrance.
- **Contemporary variant**: in cities the "body-farewell" and "crackers along the way" partly take over the function of the roadside offering.
- **Ethnic minorities**: the Miao set "blocking-road wine" on the way to send off and greet; the Yi sing "pointing the road" on the way; the Tibetan sky-burial sets no roadside offering. The roadside offering mainly exists among the Han and some southern peoples.

## 5. Objects and Documents

- **Incense table, incense, candles, fruit, wine**: the roadside-offering display.
- **Paper money, firecrackers**: for burning and firing.
- **Mourning couplets, sacrificial hangings**: gifts from the offerer.
- **Ritual book**: records the number of offerings and the offerers.
- **Thanks note**: sent afterward in acknowledgement.
