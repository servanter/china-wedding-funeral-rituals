# Announcing the Death and Obituary Notices

## 1. Definition

**Announcing the death** is telling kin and friends the news; the **obituary** (also "funeral notice", "obituary card") is the written document used. "Fu" is cognate with "to hurry", taking the sense of rushing to tell.

An obituary must state: the dead person's name and status, the date of death (some areas the birth and death), the funeral schedule (dressing, public mourning, burial), the mourning residence address and contact, and the signing kin (in order of mourning grade).

The obituary's wording follows strict rank and form — anciently the "words of hurrying-to-tell", core of the mourning document system; see the "document templates / obituary template" entry.

## 2. Historical Origins

- The *Liji* ("Zaji") fixes the wording: "to announce to one's lord, say 'your servant So-and-so is dead'; for parents, wife, eldest son, 'your servant So-and-so's So-and-so is dead' " — fixed addressee and self-reference.
- The *Yili* ("Shisangli"): "then hurry to the lord" — the gentleman first tells his lord, the first ritual priority.
- The *Liji* ("Tan'gong") records differing announcements by relation (e.g., "father's friend"), showing closeness gradation.
- From the Han the obituary became common. Tang-Song had "obituary cards", Ming-Qing "obituary notices" with fixed form, opening "Unfilial son So-and-so, heavy in sin, not having destroyed himself, misfortune extends to our late father…".
- Modern: newspaper obituaries, then electronic and group-chat obituaries.
- The act of announcing itself is customary: the filial son must kneel to tell, not enter another's door (tell at the threshold) — varying by place.

## 3. Ceremonial Procedure

1. **First tell the lord (or superior)**: ritually the gentleman first announces to the lord; later, the work unit, superior or clan elder.
2. **Then tell clan kin**: send a specialist to notify the clan, in order of mourning closeness.
3. **Draft the obituary**: name (taboo), date of death, age, funeral schedule, residence, signing kin.
4. **Deliver**: by hand ("announcing card"), posting, newspaper, or electronic obituary.
5. **Receive**: kin on receiving go to condole, or send letters/telegrams, wreaths, banners.
6. **Filial son answers**: to those who come, the filial son kneels to thank (folk custom).

## 4. Regional and Ethnic Variants

- **North**: "door paper", "white paper" posted to announce; the announcer may not enter, but calls at the threshold.
- **Jiang-Zhe**: deliver "announcing cards"; some hang white cloth or white lantern at the door.
- **Min-Tai**: "announce white", post white paper, hang "filial lamp"; the announcer carries a white card.
- Some places: the announcer wears a hat and carries an umbrella ("shading the sky"), and must not wear red.
- **Ethnic difference**: Muslim peoples (Hui, Uyghur) bury the same or next day, announce by word or phone quickly, **with no lying-in-state or public-mourning**, so the obituary's form and function differ; some Tibetan areas notify by inviting monks and chanting instead.

## 5. Objects and Documents

- **Obituary (notice, card)**: the core document, white paper, fixed form.
- **Door paper / white paper**: posted at the door to announce.
- **White lantern, filial lamp**: mark the mourning house.
- **Condolence letter, telegram, wreath, banner**: the condolers' answers.
- **Gift ledger**: registers condolers and gifts.
