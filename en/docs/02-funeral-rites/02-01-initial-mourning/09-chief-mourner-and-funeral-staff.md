# Appointing the Chief Mourner and the Funeral Staff

## 1. Definition

The **chief mourner** (*sang zhu*) is the one who presides over the mourning, normally the dead person's eldest son; if he is dead, the "weight-bearing grandson" (*cheng zhong*) acts. The chief mourner presides at the sacrifice, receives condolers and leads the rites — the central figure of all ceremonies.

The **funeral staff** assists the chief mourner. The Song *Shuyi* and *Family Rituals* regularised the division: the *hu sang* (overseer), the *si shu* (scribe), the *si huo* (quartermaster), plus the rite-master and the usher.

## 2. Historical Origins

- The *Yili* ("Shisangli") calls the chief mourner "the host" (*zhu ren*): "the host bows, touches the ground" — the eldest son. The office is one with the lineage system and the five degrees of mourning: the great line sacrifices, the small lines divide; in mourning the eldest presides.
- The *Liji* ("Sangfu si zhi"): "mourning becomes rite because the affection is thick and the dress heavy; hence for the father, untrimmed mourning three years" — the chief mourner's dress is heaviest, his status highest.
- If the dead has no son, an heir is chosen ("adoption") to preside — a matter of inheritance, treated with great care.
- The Song *Shuyi* first systematically lists the staff: *hu sang* governs all; *si shu* handles obituary, rite-books, elegies; *si huo* handles finances — the origin of the later "manager-accountant".
- Ming-Qing folk formed a mature division: manager (*da lao*), accountant, rite-master, usher, kitchen, gravedigger, coffin-shop.
- Modern: professional funeral services outsource some functions to funeral homes and companies.

## 3. Ceremonial Procedure

1. **Fix the chief mourner**: by law or rite, the eldest son (or weight-bearing grandson, or heir).
2. **Fix the hostess**: the chief mourner's wife (or the dead's wife, the eldest daughter-in-law), presiding within, receiving women guests.
3. **Invite the overseer**: a respected, rite-knowing clansman or villager as *hu sang* (manager), governing all.
4. **Set the accountant (*si huo*)**: manage income/outgo, gift registry, purchasing.
5. **Set the scribe (*si shu*)**: obituary, gift-book, elegy, couplets, thank-cards.
6. **Invite the rite-master**: preside at the family offering (three-libation), dotting the tablet, taking dress.
7. **Set the usher**: receive condolers, guide, incense, registry.
8. **Set the kitchen**: prepare the mourning feast.
9. **Chief mourner's work**: keep vigil, wail, receive and answer condolers, head the obituary signatures.

## 4. Regional and Ethnic Variants

- **North (Jing-Jin)**: a "grand manager"; old Beijing-Tianjin had "coffin-shops" and "shed-shops" for carrying, sheds, insignia.
- **Jiang-Zhe**: "accountant gentleman", "master of ceremony", "mourning simple, rites not lacking".
- **Min-Tai**: "master" (Daoist), "rite-master", "earth officer", and the "grand-aunt" roles, finely divided.
- **Old Shanghai**: the "funeral home" as a modern trade, commercialising the division.
- **Ethnic minorities**: mostly by clan elders or religious specialists — the Hui by the ahong, Tibetans by lamas, Yi/Miao by *bimo*/*zhai lao*, Naxi by *dongba* — a structure different from the Han "chief mourner + overseer".

## 5. Objects and Documents

- **Gift-book**: registers condolers and gifts, the accountant's basis.
- **Obituary**: written by the scribe, headed by the chief mourner.
- **Account book**: held by the quartermaster.
- **Mourning dress, filial staff**: prepared by chief mourner and filial kin.
- **Thank-card**: the after-funeral answer (see the thank-card template).
