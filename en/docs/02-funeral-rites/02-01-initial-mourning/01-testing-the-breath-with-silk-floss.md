# Testing the Breath with Silk Floss (Shukuang) and Changing the Mat

## 1. Definition

*Shukuang* is the rite by which the ancients ascertained death. *Shu* means "to place", and *kuang* is fresh floss. A wisp of new floss was laid upon the mouth and nostrils of the dying to see whether it stirred with the breath: if the floss did not move, life was judged to have ceased.

Accompanying it is *yizé* ("changing the mat"): the sick person was moved from the inner bedchamber to the bed of the main chamber (or to the floor). A man ending his life in the main chamber, a woman in the inner chamber, was the mark of a "good death" (*shanzhong*), and is the origin of the phrase *shouzhong zhengqin* ("to die a natural death in one's own home").

## 2. Historical Origins

- The *Liji* ("Sangdaji", Records of Mourning) says "place floss to await the cessation of breath" — the earliest explicit record of *shukuang*.
- The *Liji* ("Tan'gong shang") records Zengzi changing the mat: gravely ill, Zengzi was found lying on a fine official's mat (a gift of Ji Sun); he reflected that "I may die only when upright", had others help him rise and change the mat, and "before the mat was reset he passed away" — establishing the dying ethic of "dying upright".
- The *Yili* ("Shisangli", Ceremony of Initial Mourning) says "die in the proper chamber, cover with the shroud"; "proper chamber" means the main chamber.
- Zhu Xi's *Family Rituals* stresses that "a man should not die in a woman's arms, nor a woman in a man's", bringing deathbed handling into the family-rite code.
- "Shukuang" became a metonym for "the point of death", as in the phrase "at the moment of *shukuang*".
- In folk practice *yizé* became *banpu* ("moving the bed") or *yichuang*; the breath-testing function of *shukuang* was replaced by medical observation of vital signs, yet its ritual meaning (dying in the main chamber) still influences custom.

## 3. Ceremonial Procedure

1. **Move to the main chamber**: as death approached, a man was moved to the main chamber, a woman to the inner chamber. Not dying there was "not a proper death", a grave taboo.
2. **Change the mat (*yizé*)**: remove the original bedding, replace it with a plain mat, and help the dying lie flat.
3. **Test the breath (*shukuang*)**: place new floss at the mouth and nostrils and watch; if it does not stir, death is confirmed.
4. **Raise the lament**: when breath ceases, the family laments. Men "bind the hair" (removing ornaments), women "remove the hairpin".
5. **Clear the bed**: remove the old clothes, set the corpse-bed, and prepare for the rite of "recall" (*fu*).
6. **Summon the officers**: invite clan elders and functionaries to assign the funeral tasks; folk custom burns paper money ("the fallen-to-the-ground paper") and fires crackers to announce the death.

## 4. Regional and Ethnic Variants

- **North (Jing-Jin-Ji-Shanxi)**: called *banpu*, moving the sick onto a temporary plank bed in the hall, commonly the "peace bed" (*taiping chuang*).
- **Jiangnan**: called *yichuang* or "onto the cool bed", usually in the centre or east of the hall.
- **Min-Tai (Fujian-Taiwan)**: called *banpu*, laying a plank in the main hall with the rule "male left, female right"; the filial son must kneel and "call the soul".
- **Sichuan**: "onto the cool bed", and a silver coin is placed in the dying person's mouth.
- **Ethnic minorities**: some peoples avoid dying on a bed (certain mountain groups) and move the person to the ground or outdoors; in some Tibetan areas lamas are invited to chant at the deathbed — a religious form of end-of-life care. Practices differ greatly; see the ethnic-minority funeral entries.

## 5. Objects and Documents

- **Floss (*kuang*)**: the breath-testing object, later a metonym for the deathbed.
- **Mat (*zé*)**: the plain mat changed in *yizé*.
- **Shroud (*lianqin*)**: the cover cloth for the corpse (*Yili*: "cover with the shroud").
- **Candle, paper money**: folk-level items.
- **Obituary draft**: some areas begin drafting the obituary at this point.
