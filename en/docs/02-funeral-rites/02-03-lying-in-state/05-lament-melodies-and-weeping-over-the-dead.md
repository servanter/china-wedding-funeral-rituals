# Lament Melodies and Weeping over the Dead

## 1. Definition

Weeping over the dead (*ku ling*) is the singing-wail before the spirit during lying-in-state; its tune is called "lament melody", "wail tune".

**Division from "Mourning Wails and Lament Songs"**: that entry centres on encoffining and the ritual classification of weeping; this one centres on lying-in-state, the **regional tunes** and the **professional wailer**.

## 2. Historical Origins

- The elegy system began in the Han ("Dew on the Leek", "Hao Li"); the Jin recorded "mourning songs" into ritual.
- After Tang-Song, local tune-systems formed, with "sung weeping".
- Ming-Qing gazetteers note "wailing at the spirit", "crying the seventh", the Xiang-E area singing by sevens.
- **Professional wailers** ("wailing women", "substitute wailers") appeared — hired for fixed words and tune, a commercialisation the literati criticised ("grief not from oneself, the wail is not one's wail"). Recorded here as custom, criticised.
- Rural areas keep it; cities largely lost it.

## 3. Ceremonial Procedure

1. **Open the tune**: the chief wailer (daughter, daughter-in-law, wife) at the spirit, high and long, calling the dead's name.
2. **Tell the tale**: by content — the life, nurture, toil, parting.
3. **Harmony**: women join, a collective wail.
4. **Timed**: at morning/evening offerings, each seventh, on visitors, the night before burial ("farewell").
5. **Cry the sevens**: progressive by seventh, ending at the seventh seventh.
6. **Farewell wail**: fiercest the night before burial.
7. **Close**: after burial, low remembrance at sacrifices.

## 4. Regional and Ethnic Variants

- **North China**: high, stern tune, "My X—" opening, falling tail.
- **Jiangnan**: gentle, Wu-dialect drawl, set "crying the seventh".
- **Xiang-E-Chuan-Qian**: fullest system, kin with "cry-marriage", tidy wording.
- **Min-Tai**: with Daoist chant, sea-air timbre.
- **Professional wailers**: Xiang, E, Chuan, Qian and parts of North — **hired wailing violates the rite's "grief from oneself"; a vulgarity.**
- **Ethnic minorities**: Yi "mourning songs", Tujia "funeral drum", Miao "funeral-song hall" are mostly **collective singing** with fixed song-halls, functioning to comfort, send the soul and carry clan history — different in organisation from the Han women-led wailing.

## 5. Objects and Documents

- **Mourning-song copies**: some areas.
- **Funeral drum, gong**: Tujia accompaniment.
- **Recordings, song collections**: intangible-heritage surveys.
- **Wailing kerchief**: for tears.
