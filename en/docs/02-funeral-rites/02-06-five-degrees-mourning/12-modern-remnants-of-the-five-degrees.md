# Modern Remnants of the Five Degrees

## 1. Definition

The five-degree system as a **state ritual** no longer exists, but three of its core ideas still run in contemporary folk:

1. **"Mourning dress has weight"**: the dress for parents must differ from that for grandparents and uncles.
2. **"The mourning period has length"**: the period for parents is clearly longer than for other kin.
3. **"Within the five degrees is close kin"**: still widely used as the oral standard of "near or far kin".

## 2. The Degrees of Modern Mourning Dress

Contemporary folk mourning dress broadly divides into three grades (corresponding to the three-level simplification of the five degrees):

| Folk grade | Corresponding degree | Wearing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Heavy mourning** | Zhan cui / Zi cui | Coarse white mourning gown, mourning cap (stiffened white cloth), hemp rope at waist, white shoes; some areas "hemp and sackcloth" |
| **Next mourning** | Da gong / Xiao gong | White mourning clothes (finer), mourning cap; no hemp rope |
| **Light mourning** | Si ma | Black armband, white flower, white cloth strip, white badge |

**Modern form of the mourning period:**

| Kin | Traditional period | Common today |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Parents | Zhan cui three years (27 months) | 100 days / a year / three years (symbolic) |
| Grandparents | Zi cui no-staff (one year) | To after the procession or on the "fifth seven" |
| Uncles and aunts | Zi cui no-staff (one year) | Removed right after the procession |
| Cousin-brothers | Da gong nine months | To the procession |
| Maternal grandparents, uncle/aunt | Xiao gong / Si ma | To the procession or black armband for some days |

**Removing mourning** (taking off mourning): mostly at "a hundred days", "a year" or "three years"; Min-Tai ends with "joining the furnace"; modern cities mostly limit it to some days after cremation to a hundred days.

## 3. Historical Origins

- **1930 Civil Code**: replaces the degree with degrees of kinship (Roman-law system); the five degrees lose civil-law effect.
- **After 1950**: funeral reform and custom-change simplified mourning dress and period; cities use black armband and white flower.
- **Since the 1990s**: some areas see a "traditional revival" — making mourning gowns (gown, cap) spreads again in villages; at the same time the "wearing mourning" form simplifies (black armband, white flower).
- **Typical surviving forms**:
  - North China: mourning gown, mourning cap, hemp rope, white shoes;
  - Min-Tai: three grades "hemp shirt — ramie shirt — white shirt", with a "filial belt" (white cloth strip);
  - Guangdong: white-cloth mourning dress, "mourning staff";
  - Cities: black armband, white flower, white cloth badge.

## 4. Regional and Ethnic Variants

- **Min-Tai (Fujian-Taiwan)**: the three-grade mourning-dress system is best preserved; "hemp shirt — ramie shirt — white shirt" maps exactly onto the heavy/next/light three grades of the five degrees — the most complete modern remnant.
- **North China**: marks by hemp rope and mourning cap; the heavy/next distinction is clear.
- **Jiangnan**: mourning dress is simpler, but genealogies and family-rite manuscripts still keep written degree records.
- **Contemporary cities**: black armband and white flower dominate; the degree difference basically vanishes, leaving only "lineal kin vs. others".
- **Ethnic minorities**: each people's mourning marks differ (mourning cloth, cutting hair, forbidding ornaments, specific-colour clothes), with no succession relation to the Han five degrees, and must be recorded separately.

## 5. Objects and Documents

- **Mourning dress**: hemp shirt (heavy), ramie shirt (next), white shirt (light); the north uses gown and cap.
- **Filial belt, white cloth strip, black armband, white flower**.
- **Mourning staff**: held by the filial son.
- **Genealogy and family-rite manuscripts**: the written carrier of degree knowledge.
- **The "degree line" in the obituary**: the obituary lists appellations like "unfilial son so-and-so, term-mourning grandson so-and-so" — the written remnant of the five degrees (see the "Funeral Appellations" section).
