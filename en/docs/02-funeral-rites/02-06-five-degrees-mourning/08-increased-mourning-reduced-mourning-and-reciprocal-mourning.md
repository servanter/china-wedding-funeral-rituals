# Increased, Reduced and Reciprocal Mourning

## 1. Definition

Beyond the "regular mourning" (*zhengfu*) of the five degrees, there are three rules that shift the degree:

| Rule | Meaning | Typical example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Increased mourning** (*jiafu*) | Grace or duty raises the degree | Increase for the maternal uncle (after the Tang); increase for the aunt; the mother's mourning elevated |
| **Reduced mourning** (*jiangfu*) | Lineage, outer-kin or special status lowers it | A married-out woman one degree below for her natal kin; the adopted heir reduced for his birth parents |
| **Reciprocal mourning** (*baofu*) | The two parties mourn each other equally | Aunt for nephew ↔ nephew for aunt; maternal uncle for nephew ↔ nephew for maternal uncle |

**"Derivative mourning"** (*congfu*): mourning worn by reason of the person one follows (e.g. the wife mourns the husband's kin by following the husband) — another special rule.

## 2. Historical Origins

- The *Yili* ("Sangfu") governs the whole degree system by six classes: "regular / increased / reduced / duty / derivative / reciprocal", an extremely precise framework.
- **Lineage meaning of reduced mourning**:
  - **A woman married out**: from a natal-line member she becomes "outer kin", her mourning for natal kin uniformly one degree lower (e.g. for parents from *zhan cui* down to *zi cui* no-staff; for grandparents from *zi cui* down to *da gong*).
  - **The adopted heir**: wears *zhan cui* three years for the father he succeeds, but reduced to *zi cui* no-staff for his birth parents — the mourning expression of "transfer of lineage" (移宗).
  - **The divorced mother**: reduced mourning for the birth mother who was repudiated.
- **History of increased mourning**: after the Tang there were increases for some outer kin (e.g. the uncle, the aunt), reflecting the trend "grace outweighs lineage".
- **Reciprocal mourning**: ensures symmetry of mourning between kin, e.g. "nephew for aunt" matches "aunt for nephew".
- The *Liji* ("Fuwen") discusses the various changed-mourning cases — the key text for understanding the system.
- Ming and Qing ritual books broadly follow the *Yili*, with more adjustment of outer-kin mourning.

## 3. Ceremonial Procedure

1. **Fix the regular mourning**: first fix the "regular mourning" by the natal nine-degree kin rank.
2. **Judge reduced mourning**:
   - Is she married out (a woman)?
   - Is he an adopted heir?
   - Is he the son of a divorced mother?
3. **Judge increased mourning**: is there a grace-weighted special case (e.g. foster mother, stepmother, nurturing mother)?
4. **Judge reciprocal and derivative mourning**: does the other party mourn me? Do I mourn by reason of the one I follow (husband, wife, son)?
5. **Assume the dress**: wear the mourning dress by the finally fixed degree.
6. **Record**: in lineage-developed areas, record the mourning change in the genealogy or family-rite manuscript.

## 4. Regional and Ethnic Variants

- The principle of "reduced mourning" still shows in folk: **a married-out daughter's mourning dress for her natal elders is generally lighter than her brothers'**.
- **Min-Tai (Fujian-Taiwan)**: a married-out daughter for her natal elders mostly wears "ramie shirt" or "white shirt" (light dress) — the folk form of "reduced mourning".
- **North China**: a married-out daughter returning to mourn mostly wears a "white mourning cap", not the heavy dress.
- **Contemporary**: the fine rules of the five degrees mostly no longer exist, but the folk idea "a married-out daughter's mourning should be a bit lighter" still lingers in some areas.
- **Ethnic minorities**: each people has its own kin-mourning rules, with a different lineage logic from "reduced" and "reciprocal" mourning, and must be recorded separately.

## 5. Objects and Documents

- **Mourning diagram (with changed-mourning marks)**.
- **Genealogy**: records whether a woman is married out, the heir's status, etc., information affecting the degree.
- **Ritual books**: *Yili* "Sangfu", *Liji* "Fuwen", *Comprehensive Rituals*.
- **Family-rite manuscripts**: records of actual folk practice.
