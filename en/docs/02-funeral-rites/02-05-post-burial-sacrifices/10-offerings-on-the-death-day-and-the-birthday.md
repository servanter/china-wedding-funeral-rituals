# Offerings on the Death Day and the Birthday

## 1. Definition

- **Death-day offering** (*jiri ji*): sacrifice on the day of the deceased's death ("death day", "taboo day"). This is a **private sacrifice**, limited to the deceased's direct descendants, small in scale and not public.
- **Birthday offering** (*shengchen ji*, "underworld birthday"): sacrifice on the deceased's birthday. This custom is less widespread than the death-day offering, done mostly when there is a specific commemorative need (e.g. celebrated figures, worthies' birth anniversaries).

The key point of the death-day offering: unlike the "seasonal public sacrifices" (Qingming, Zhongyuan, winter solstice), it is **a single family or clan's private sacrifice to a single person**.

## 2. Historical Origins

- The *Liji* ("Jiyi"): "the gentleman has a lifelong mourning, which is the death day" — taking the death day as a "lifelong mourning", showing its weight.
- The *Liji* ("Tan'gong"): "on the death day no music" — the ritual rule that no music be played on the death day.
- The *Liji* ("Yuzao"): "on the zi-mao days, millet food and vegetable broth" — dietary limits tied to the death day.
- Zhu Xi's *Family Rituals* brings the death-day offering into the family rites, prescribing "on the death day set the seat in the main chamber, arrange the vessels, prepare the feast, and the host and below change dress (to plain)" and perform a simplified sacrifice.
- Ming and Qing gazetteers record "on the death day set the sacrifice, the descendants all gather, no feasting or music".
- Contemporary death-day offerings are still widespread, simplified to lighting incense, serving a meal and burning paper at home (or before the grave); some go to the cemetery on the death day.

## 3. Ceremonial Procedure

1. **Choose the time**: the death day itself (mostly from morning to evening; some only in the morning).
2. **Change dress**: participants wear plain-coloured clothes, avoid bright colours (old rite required plain dress; today mostly relaxed).
3. **Set the seat**: in the main chamber (or hall) set the deceased's tablet or portrait, the offering table.
4. **Arrange vessels and feast**: set offerings — wine, rice, dishes, fruit, cakes (insist on the food the deceased liked in life).
5. **Light incense**: the head of household (or eldest son) lights incense.
6. **Kneel**: the family by seniority in turn kneels.
7. **Read the prayer**: some families read the death-day prayer (recounting the deceased's virtue and deeds).
8. **Burn paper**: burn paper money.
9. **No feasting or music**: on the death day no music, no entertaining guests; some areas insist "no meat on the death day" or "eat vegetarian".
10. **Remove offerings**: after the rite, remove; the offerings are shared (the "eating of blessing").
11. **Birthday offering (if performed)**: the rite as above, but the mood slightly eased from the death day; offerings add longevity noodles and longevity peaches.

## 4. Regional and Ethnic Variants

- **North China**: the death-day offering mostly before the home tablet, insisting "on the death day no needlework, no music".
- **Jiangnan**: death-day sacrifice, insisting on "offering what they liked in life", with the tradition of reading the prayer.
- **Min-Tai (Fujian-Taiwan)**: the death-day offering is "doing the taboo", set before the ancestral tablet, on the day "no feasting, no congratulating"; the birthday offering is "doing the birthday", set on the underworld birthday.
- **Guangdong**: "doing the death day", insisting on serving a meal and burning paper; the first three years' death days of the newly dead are more solemn.
- **Ethnic minorities**: some peoples have their own death-day sacrifice, but more centre on collective ancestor days; Muslim peoples on set days after a relative's death (e.g. the fortieth day, the anniversary) recite the *Quran* and give alms — a religious act, not a "death-day home sacrifice". The form of the death-day offering must be understood by each people's religion and family system.

## 5. Objects and Documents

- **Tablet, portrait, offering table, incense burner, candle stand**.
- **Offerings**: wine, rice, dishes, fruit, cakes; birthday offering adds longevity noodles and peaches.
- **Paper money**.
- **Death-day prayer**: document.
- **Lineage register**: records the death day (registers mostly carry each member's "birth and death dates").
- **Family-rite books**: Zhu Xi's *Family Rituals* and the like.
