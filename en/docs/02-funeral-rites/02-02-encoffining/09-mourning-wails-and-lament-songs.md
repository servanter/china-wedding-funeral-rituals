# Mourning Wails and Lament Songs

## 1. Definition

Wailing (*ku sang*) is the ritual weeping at funerals. In the rites, weeping has **set manner, time and vocal grade**:

- *Ku*: sound and tears.
- *Qi*: tears, no sound.
- *Hao*: loud crying.
- *Yong*: weeping with stamping — the fiercest.

The mourning song (*ku sang ge*) is the rhymed utterance sung in wailing, formalising grief into rhythmic words. Folk hold that "good wailing" honours the dead and publicly proves filial piety.

## 2. Historical Origins

- The *Liji* ("Tan'gong"): "at first death, one is full, as if at an end" — shapes of grief; "on days of weeping, no music".
- The *Liji* ("Sangdaji") fixes times: "at first passing, the host wails, brothers weep, women wail and leap" — by status.
- The *Yili* ("Shisangli") fixes places: host (male) east, hostess (female) west.
- The elegy tradition is early: the *Shijing* "Yellow Bird" is its spring; the Han "Dew on the Leek", "Hao Li" (by Tian Heng's followers) became common elegies; the *Jinshu* ("Treatise on Rites") records the "mourning song" system.
- After Tang-Song, folk wailing became tunes ("cry-tune", "cry-song"); Ming-Qing gazetteers note "mourning songs", "elegies", "spirit-wail tunes".
- Professionalisation appeared: hired wailers ("wailing women", "substitute wailers") — a vulgarity to be criticised.

## 3. Ceremonial Procedure

1. **Wail at first death**: on breath's end, the family laments; men bind hair, women remove pins; host wails, brothers weep, women wail and leap.
2. **Wail at encoffining**: fiercest (Yili: "without count"); each dress added, a wail.
3. **Morning/evening wail**: by rule at the offerings; on guests' condoling.
4. **Wail places**: men east, women west, not mixed.
5. **Mourning song**: led by the chief wailer (daughter, daughter-in-law, wife), joined by others, by content: cry for parents (nurture), cry for spouse (marriage toil), cry at the spirit (the dead's life), cry to send (at procession), cry the arrangement (folk oversight of propriety).
6. **End wailing**: after burial, low remembrance at sacrifices.

## 4. Regional and Ethnic Variants

- **North China**: high, piercing tune, fixed "My dear X—" opening.
- **Jiang-Zhe**: gentle, tidy wording, "cry the seventh", "cry the spirit".
- **Xiang-E-Chuan-Qian**: most developed, kin with "cry-marriage"; a full "cry" system (cf. the "cry-marriage" entry).
- **Min-Tai**: sea-air tune, with Daoist chanting.
- **Ethnic minorities**: Yi "mourning songs", Miao "funeral songs", Tujia "funeral-drum songs" each form a system with their own myth (ancestor migration, the soul's road home), functioning beyond lyric to **guide the way and carry clan history** — to be recorded apart from Han wailing.

## 5. Objects and Documents

- **No specific object**: the human voice is core.
- **Mourning-song copies**: some areas, in *China's Ballad Corpus*.
- **Spirit tablet, coffin setting**: the space of wailing.
- **Recordings**: modern intangible-heritage surveys.
